**A 3D printing pen is a handheld tool that melts plastic filament and pushes it out of a hot nozzle, so you can draw lines that harden in seconds – on paper, on an object or straight up in the air.** It works on the same idea as an FDM 3D printer, but your hand replaces the motors and the software. 3D pens are used for crafts, school projects, models, jewellery and small repairs to 3D prints. This guide explains how a 3D printer pen works, the three main types, which filament to use, safety and age guidance, how to use one step by step, project ideas and how a pen compares with a real 3D printer. Facts come from pen makers' guides and filament makers, checked November 2026.

## 3D printing pens at a glance

| | Hot pen | Low-temperature pen | Cold (UV resin) pen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material | PLA, ABS (some also wood, flexible, nylon) | PCL | Liquid photopolymer resin |
| How it sets | Cools in air | Cools in air | Cured by a UV light in the pen |
| Working temperature | Tip can reach around 240 °C | PCL melts near 60 °C | No heating |
| Typical users | Teens, adults, hobbyists | Younger children | Younger children |
| Strength of result | Hardest, most durable | Soft, slightly flexible | Depends on the resin |
| Main care point | Burns, ABS fumes | Low burn risk; still warm | Avoid skin contact with uncured resin |

## How a 3D pen works

Inside a hot 3D pen there is a small motor, a heater and a metal nozzle. You push a strand of filament into the back, the motor feeds it forward, the heater melts it, and soft plastic comes out of the tip. It is the same extrusion process an FDM printer uses, only guided by hand. All3DP compares it to a hot glue gun, which is a fair picture of how it feels.

The line stays soft for a moment and then hardens as it cools. That gives you a few seconds to shape it, join it to another line or pull it upward into a free-standing strand. Most pens have buttons for extrude and unload, a speed control and either a temperature setting or a preset for each material. Power comes from a cable, USB or a built-in battery.

**Tip:** no software or files are needed. You plug the pen in, load filament and draw.

## Hot, low-temperature and cold pens

**Hot pens** melt PLA or ABS. One maker, 3Doodler, says the metal tip of its Create pen can reach about 240 °C. They give the strongest, cleanest lines and the widest choice of filament, but the tip can burn skin.

**Low-temperature ("warm") pens** use PCL (polycaprolactone), a plastic that melts at about 60 °C. Filament makers list nozzle temperatures around 75–85 °C for PCL, far below PLA. Burn risk is much lower, which is why these pens are sold for younger children. The trade-off: PCL stays slightly soft and flexible, and it is weaker than PLA.

**Cold pens** contain liquid photosensitive resin instead of filament. A UV light near the tip hardens the resin as it comes out – a hand-held version of resin 3D printing. Nothing gets hot, but uncured resin should not stay on skin, so follow the maker's handling advice.

## Which filament to use in a 3D pen

| Filament | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| PLA | Everyday drawing, models, gifts | Plant-based, low odour, the usual first choice |
| ABS | Strong, slightly heat-resistant parts; repairs to ABS items | Needs higher heat and gives off fumes – ventilate |
| PCL | Low-temperature kids' pens | Melts near 60 °C; soft and less durable; can be reshaped in hot water |
| Specialty (wood, flexible, nylon, metal-look) | Advanced pens only | Check the pen supports it |

Most hot pens take **1.75 mm** filament, the same diameter many 3D printers use, so leftover printer PLA often works. Some pens, especially kids' models, accept only the maker's own refill sticks. Always check the manual: filament that is the wrong diameter or material can jam the pen.

**Tip:** to repair a broken object, match the filament to the object. PLA bonds best to PLA and ABS to ABS. For PLA and food contact, see [Is PLA food safe?](/blog/is-pla-food-safe/) – pen drawings are not food-safe items.

## Safety and age guidance

- **Follow the age on the box.** Makers rate pens differently. 3Doodler, for example, rates its low-temperature Start pen and filament for ages 6 and up, says the Start pen has no hot parts, and says its hot Create pen is not a toy and is for ages 14 and up.
- **Treat the nozzle like a soldering iron.** On a hot pen the metal tip and fresh plastic can burn. Never touch the tip, and rest the pen on its stand, not on fabric or paper.
- **Ventilate when using ABS.** ABS gives off fumes and a strong smell when melted; work near an open window. PLA has a much milder smell.
- **Use a heat-safe surface.** Draw on paper, a silicone mat or a non-stick sheet, not directly on a table.
- **Supervise children**, even with low-temperature pens, and unplug the pen after use.
- **Uncured resin** from cold pens should be wiped off skin straight away.

## How to use a 3D printing pen, step by step

1. **Set up:** plug in or charge the pen, switch it on and choose the material setting that matches your filament.
2. **Heat:** wait for the ready light before pushing any filament in.
3. **Load:** cut the filament end flat with scissors, push it into the back of the pen and hold extrude until plastic flows from the tip.
4. **Start on paper:** put a template or stencil under a sheet of paper or a clear mat. Begin each line with a small dot to anchor it.
5. **Draw slowly:** keep the tip close to the surface and match your hand speed to the extrusion speed. Too fast and the line breaks; too slow and it blobs.
6. **End a line:** stop extruding, hold still for a few seconds, then lift away.
7. **Build in 3D:** draw each flat side separately, peel them off, then stand them up and weld the edges together with small dots and a line over each seam.
8. **Draw in the air:** make a large anchor dot, lift the pen while extruding slowly, and hold still until the strand hardens. A gentle breath or small fan speeds cooling.
9. **Unload:** use the unload button while the pen is still hot, then switch off.

**Tip:** where two lines cross, pause a moment so the hot tip slightly remelts the first line. The joint becomes much stronger.

## 3D pen project ideas

- **Name tags, keyrings and zip pulls** – flat shapes drawn over a printed outline.
- **Wire-frame shapes** – cubes, pyramids and the Eiffel Tower built from flat panels.
- **Jewellery** – earrings, pendants and rings drawn around a pencil or dowel.
- **Phone stands and small holders** – built from flat parts welded together.
- **School models** – molecules, cells, bridges and architectural models.
- **Decorating 3D prints** – adding raised details, initials or texture to a printed part.
- **Repairs** – filling small gaps, fixing broken clips or reinforcing a weak joint on a 3D print, in the same material.

## 3D pen vs 3D printer

| | 3D printing pen | 3D printer |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Your hand | Motors following a file |
| Software | None | Slicer (and often CAD) |
| Accuracy | Freehand, uneven | Repeatable to fractions of a millimetre |
| Size of objects | Small to medium sketches | Up to the build volume |
| Time to start | Minutes | Setup, levelling, slicing |
| Best for | Art, crafts, learning, small repairs | Functional parts, copies, detailed models |

A pen is a good way to understand how layers of melted plastic build up an object, and a natural step before a printer. ## What to look for when choosing a 3D pen

1. **Who will use it:** a low-temperature PCL or UV pen for young children; a hot PLA/ABS pen for teens and adults.
2. **Filament:** a pen that takes standard 1.75 mm PLA and ABS gives you the widest and easiest refill choice.
3. **Controls:** adjustable speed and temperature, plus a clear extrude and unload button.
4. **Power:** corded pens can run for long sessions; battery pens are easier to carry.
5. **Nozzle protection and a stand:** especially in a family or classroom.
6. **Accessories:** a silicone mat and stencils make the first projects much easier.

## Verdict

A 3D printing pen is a simple, hands-on way to draw in three dimensions with no software. Choose a low-temperature or UV pen for young children, and a hot pen that takes 1.75 mm PLA and ABS for older users who want stronger results and more materials. Use it for art, school projects and quick repairs, and move to a 3D printer when you need accurate, repeatable parts.
