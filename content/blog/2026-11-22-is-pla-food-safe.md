**PLA as a plastic can be food-safe, but a 3D-printed PLA part usually is not.** The problem is not the material but the way FDM printing builds a part: in layers, with tiny grooves between them where food collects and bacteria grow. Washing does not reach into those grooves reliably.

This guide follows Prusa Research’s published guidance. If you make items for sale or for others, check your local food-contact rules.

## The four things that decide food safety

| Factor | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Filament | Certified food-contact filament; natural (uncoloured) is safest because pigments and additives vary |
| Colourant | Some brands certify only certain colours |
| Nozzle | Stainless steel; brass wears and can leave traces |
| Surface | A certified food-safe coating (for example, a food-grade epoxy) to seal layer lines |

## Why layer lines are the real problem

Even with a perfect, certified filament, the surface of a print has gaps between layers. Prusa’s test of untreated, smoothed and epoxy-coated PLA cups showed why untreated prints are risky. Soap and detergent can also soak into the layer lines and be released later.

## Safer ways to use prints in the kitchen

- Print **tools that do not touch food**: holders, organisers, clips and stands.
- Use prints as **moulds or cutters once**, then throw them away.
- **Coat** the food-contact surface with a certified food-safe coating and follow the coating’s curing instructions.
- For containers, cups and bottles, **buy food-grade products** instead. Prusa does not recommend 3D prints as food containers.
