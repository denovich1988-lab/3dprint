**PLA is the easiest filament to print and the best choice for detailed, decorative parts; PETG is tougher and more heat-resistant, which makes it better for functional parts.** Prusa Research sums it up as “PLA for pretty things and PETG for functional things”. That rule works for most beginners.

Temperatures below come from Prusa’s material guide. Always start with the profile in your slicer for your exact filament brand.

## At a glance

| | PLA | PETG |
|---|---|---|
| Nozzle temperature | 185–235 °C | 215–270 °C |
| Bed temperature | 50–60 °C | 70–90 °C |
| Ease of printing | Very easy | Easy, with more stringing |
| Toughness | Stiff, can be brittle | Tough, flexes before breaking |
| Heat resistance | Softens in a hot car | Better than PLA |
| Bridges and overhangs | Very good | Usually worse than PLA |
| Typical uses | Figures, models, prototypes, cosplay | Brackets, clips, outdoor parts, mechanical parts |

## When to use PLA

Use PLA for anything you look at more than you load: figures, vases, display models, prototypes and props. It prints fast, needs little tuning, sticks well to most beds and gives crisp detail and good overhangs.

## When to use PETG

Use PETG for parts that bend, take impacts, get warm or live outdoors: hooks, brackets, cable clips, planters and enclosures. Prusa uses PETG for many of its own printer parts.

## Printing tips

- **Bed adhesion.** PETG can stick too well to smooth PEI. Prusa’s guide suggests glue stick as a release layer on some sheets.
- **Stringing.** PETG strings more. Use your slicer’s PETG profile, dry the filament, and adjust retraction only if needed.
- **Cooling.** PLA likes strong part cooling; PETG prints better with less fan.
- **Enclosure.** Neither needs one. Prusa notes that printing PLA or PETG inside a hot enclosure can hurt cooling.
