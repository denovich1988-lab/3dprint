<h2>What is FDM in 3D printing?</h2>
<p>FDM is short for <strong>fused deposition modeling</strong>. Scott Crump, co-founder of Stratasys, developed the process in the late 1980s, and Stratasys still holds the trademark on the name &quot;FDM&quot;. When the key patents expired around 2009, the open-source RepRap project and a wave of hobby companies started building cheap filament printers. Because they could not use the trademarked name, RepRap adopted <strong>fused filament fabrication (FFF)</strong> as a free, generic term.</p>
<p>In practice FDM and FFF describe the same process, and most people say &quot;FDM printer&quot; for any filament printer, whatever the brand. Standards bodies group it under the broader category of material extrusion. Prusa&#39;s glossary, for example, defines FFF as the general term for this kind of printing and lists FDM as the other common name.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> when you read &quot;FFF printer&quot; on a spec sheet, it is not a different technology – it is an FDM-style filament printer.</p>
<h2>How an FDM 3D printer works</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Model.</strong> You start with a 3D model, usually an STL or 3MF file you design yourself or download. You can check a file in a viewer before printing.</li>
<li><strong>Slicing.</strong> A slicer program (for example PrusaSlicer, Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer, Cura or Creality Print) cuts the model into horizontal layers and works out each nozzle path, plus walls, infill and supports. It saves the result as G-code – a list of moves, temperatures and fan speeds.</li>
<li><strong>Heating.</strong> The printer heats the nozzle to the filament&#39;s printing temperature and, in most cases, heats the bed so the first layer sticks.</li>
<li><strong>Extruding.</strong> A motor-driven gear in the extruder grips the filament and pushes it into the hotend, where it melts and squeezes out of the nozzle as a thin bead.</li>
<li><strong>Drawing each layer.</strong> The motion system moves the nozzle relative to the bed to trace the outline and fill of the current layer. A part-cooling fan blows on the fresh plastic so it sets quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Stacking layers.</strong> When a layer is done, the bed drops (or the head rises) by one layer height and the next layer is drawn on top, fusing to the one below.</li>
<li><strong>Finishing.</strong> After the print cools you remove it from the build plate and break off any support material. Most parts need little or no further work.</li>
</ol>
<p>The nozzle size and layer height set how fine the print looks. A 0.4 mm nozzle is the usual default on consumer printers (Bambu Lab&#39;s A1 and P1S, for example, ship with 0.4 mm nozzles and offer 0.2, 0.6 and 0.8 mm options). Smaller nozzles and thinner layers give more detail but take longer; larger ones print faster and stronger but coarser.</p>

<p><strong>Direct drive vs Bowden.</strong> On a direct-drive printer the extruder sits on the print head, right above the hotend, which gives better control of soft filaments such as TPU. Prusa notes that delta printers need a Bowden extruder, and that this limits which filaments they handle well.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> the cheapest wear part is the nozzle. Fibre-filled filaments (carbon fibre, glass fibre) wear out brass nozzles quickly, so use a hardened steel nozzle for them.</p>

<p>The Prusa MK4 and Bambu Lab A1 are well-known bedslingers; the Prusa Core One, Bambu Lab P1S and Creality K2 Plus are CoreXY machines. Most new enclosed, high-speed printers use CoreXY, while open-frame bedslingers remain common at entry-level prices. You can line up motion systems and build volumes side by side in our <a href="/printers/compare/">printer comparison tool</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you plan to print ABS or ASA regularly, a CoreXY printer with a built-in enclosure saves you from adding one later.</p>
<h2>What materials can an FDM 3D printer use?</h2>
<p>The range of filaments is FDM&#39;s biggest advantage. Bambu Lab&#39;s filament guide splits them by how demanding they are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Easy on any printer:</strong> PLA (the default for beginners: low warping, no heated chamber needed), PETG (tougher and more heat-resistant than PLA, a little stringy) and TPU (flexible, best on direct-drive printers).</li>
<li><strong>Need an enclosure:</strong> ABS and ASA (stronger heat resistance; ASA also resists UV outdoors), PC and nylon (PA). Bambu Lab marks these as not recommended on open-frame printers because they warp and split without warm, still air.</li>
<li><strong>Need a hardened nozzle:</strong> carbon-fibre and glass-fibre filled versions of PLA, PETG, nylon and others. They are stiffer but abrasive.</li>
<li><strong>Industrial only:</strong> high-temperature plastics such as PEEK and PEI (ULTEM) need very hot nozzles and heated chambers found on professional machines.</li>
</ul>
<p>Most filaments, especially nylon, PETG and TPU, absorb moisture from the air, which causes stringing and weak prints, so keep spools dry.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> start with PLA. When you need a part that survives a hot car or outdoor sun, move to PETG or ASA rather than buying a new printer.</p>

<p>Formlabs, which makes both resin and powder printers, sums FDM up as the low-cost, easy option for simple parts, with the trade-off of lower detail and more visible layer lines. Prusa&#39;s overview gives resin layer heights of 25–100 microns and notes that uncured resin is toxic, so it should not touch skin and its vapours should not be inhaled. SLS machines are mostly industrial and far more expensive; see our <a href="/blog/sls-3d-printer/">SLS 3D printer guide</a> for how that process works.</p>
<p><strong>Which is better, FDM or SLA?</strong> For brackets, enclosures, tools, cosplay pieces and anything large or functional, FDM is usually the better fit. For tiny, highly detailed models, resin wins. Many makers end up owning one of each.</p>
<h2>Pros and cons of FDM 3D printers</h2>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Lowest entry cost and cheapest material per part of the three main technologies.</li>
<li>Clean workflow: no resin, solvents or loose powder; prints are ready after support removal.</li>
<li>Broad choice of plastics, from easy PLA to flexible TPU and fibre-reinforced nylon.</li>
<li>Large build volumes are possible at reasonable cost – see our <a href="/blog/large-3d-printer/">large 3D printer guide</a>.</li>
<li>Multi-colour and multi-material printing is now common on consumer machines.</li>
<li>Easy to repair and upgrade, with a large community and many open-source designs.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Layer lines are visible, and small text or fine textures lose detail.</li>
<li>Parts are weakest between layers, which matters for loaded parts; orient them so the load runs along the layers.</li>
<li>Overhangs need supports, which leave marks.</li>
<li>Warp-prone materials need an enclosure and a well-prepared bed.</li>
<li>FDM parts are generally not watertight without design and setting changes or sealing.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How to choose an FDM 3D printer – checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Decide what you will print.</strong> Mostly PLA and PETG toys, organisers and household parts – any modern printer will do. Regular ABS, ASA or nylon – choose an enclosed printer.</li>
<li><strong>Pick a build volume.</strong> Around 250 × 250 mm covers most projects; go smaller for a desk or larger for helmets and cosplay. Check the Z height too.</li>
<li><strong>Choose a motion system.</strong> Bedslinger for a low budget and open-frame simplicity; CoreXY for speed and an easy enclosure.</li>
<li><strong>Check the convenience features.</strong> Automatic bed levelling, a removable flexible build plate, filament run-out sensor and a direct-drive extruder save a lot of frustration for beginners.</li>
<li><strong>Think about colour.</strong> If you want multi-colour prints, check whether the printer supports a filament changer and how much filament it purges per colour change.</li>
<li><strong>Look at the software.</strong> A well-maintained slicer with good profiles matters as much as the hardware. Check whether the printer works offline over SD card or LAN if you do not want a cloud account.</li>
<li><strong>Check temperatures and nozzle options.</strong> A hotter nozzle and a hardened nozzle option open up engineering and fibre-filled filaments later.</li>
<li><strong>Plan the space.</strong> Allow room for the bed to travel on a bedslinger, a sturdy table, a spool and ventilation for fumes.</li>
<li><strong>Set the budget.</strong> Our <a href="/blog/cheap-3d-printer/">cheap 3D printer guide</a> explains what budget models include and what they leave out.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>An FDM 3D printer is the most practical first 3D printer for most people: it is affordable to buy and run, prints useful parts in many plastics, and needs no messy post-processing. Choose a bedslinger if you want the lowest cost and print mainly PLA and PETG, or an enclosed CoreXY printer if you want more speed and plan to use ABS, ASA or nylon. If you need fine detail above everything else, look at resin printing instead; for strong, support-free production parts, SLS is the professional option.</p>
