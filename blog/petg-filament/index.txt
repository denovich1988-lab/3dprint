27:T2650,<p><strong>PETG filament is a tough, slightly flexible 3D printing plastic that handles more heat than PLA and warps far less than ABS, which makes it the usual choice for functional parts.</strong> It prints on almost any printer with a heated bed and a hot end that reaches about 250 °C. This guide explains what PETG is, what it is good and bad at, typical settings, the common problems (stringing and bed adhesion) and how variants such as PETG HF differ. Facts come from Prusa&#39;s knowledge base, the Bambu Lab wiki and Polymaker, checked November 2026. Your spool label and your slicer&#39;s profile always come first.</p>

<h2>What is PETG?</h2>
<p>PETG is a polyester, from the same family as the PET used for drink bottles and food containers. The &quot;G&quot; means the polymer is modified with glycol: part of the standard building block is swapped for a bulkier one, which stops the plastic crystallising as readily. Prusa&#39;s guide says this glycol modification makes PETG less brittle and more transparent, and Hubs notes it also makes the material easier to print than plain PET.</p>
<p><strong>What does PETG stand for?</strong> Polyethylene terephthalate glycol-modified. You will also see &quot;PET-G&quot; or simply &quot;PET&quot; on some spools; check the label, because a few PET grades print differently.</p>
<h2>PETG properties – what it is good at</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Toughness.</strong> PETG bends before it breaks. Prusa describes it as tough and flexible, so parts often survive drops and snap-fits that would crack PLA.</li>
<li><strong>Heat resistance.</strong> Polymaker gives a glass transition of about 80–85 °C for PETG versus about 60 °C for PLA. Prusa says PETG parts suit most exterior use as long as they stay below 80 °C.</li>
<li><strong>Low warping.</strong> PETG has very little thermal expansion, so large flat parts stay down without an enclosure.</li>
<li><strong>Layer adhesion.</strong> Layers fuse well, which helps with watertight parts such as planters and containers.</li>
<li><strong>Water and weather.</strong> PETG resists water and humidity once printed. Hubs and Creality both describe its UV resistance as moderate, so it suits outdoor parts better than PLA, though ASA is the stronger choice for long-term sun.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if a PLA part sags in a hot car or breaks at a clip, reprint it in PETG before reaching for an engineering material.</p>
<h2>Where PETG falls short</h2>
<p>Prusa lists the downsides plainly: PETG strings easily, bridges and overhangs are worse than with PLA, fine detail is softer, it bonds very strongly to some beds and supports are harder to remove. Hubs adds that it resists most chemical smoothing, so finishing is usually sanding. It is also stiffer and less heat-resistant than ABS, ASA or PC, so it is not the answer for parts near engines or heaters.</p>
<p>If you are deciding between the two most common filaments, our <a href="/blog/petg-vs-pla/">PETG vs PLA comparison</a> goes through strength, temperature and use cases side by side.</p>

Read <a href="/blog/is-pla-food-safe/">is PLA food safe?</a> for the details, which apply to PETG too.</p>

<p><strong>Cooling trade-off.</strong> Prusa says part cooling keeps details crisp and cuts stringing, but for maximum toughness you can turn the fan off, or run it off for the first layers and at half power after.</p>
<p><strong>Enclosed printers.</strong> Bambu Lab recommends taking off the upper glass cover when the bed is above 70 °C, so heat does not build up and soften the part.</p>
<h2>Bed adhesion: stop PETG sticking too well</h2>
<p>PETG&#39;s problem is often the opposite of PLA&#39;s: it can bond so strongly that it tears pieces out of a smooth PEI sheet. Prusa advises a powder-coated (textured) or satin sheet for PETG and, if you must use smooth PEI, a layer of glue stick before printing as a release agent. Bambu Lab lists glue stick or liquid glue for PETG on its plates and does not recommend the Cool Plate.</p>
<ol>
<li>Clean the plate with dish soap and water or isopropyl alcohol.</li>
<li>Use a textured PEI sheet if you have one.</li>
<li>On smooth PEI or glass, apply a thin glue stick layer.</li>
<li>Let the bed cool fully before removing the part.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Stringing and moisture</h2>
<p>Some stringing is normal for PETG. Prusa suggests more retraction and a slightly lower nozzle temperature. But the most common cause of sudden heavy stringing, popping or rough walls is moisture: Bambu Lab&#39;s wiki says absorbed water turns to steam in the nozzle and causes stringing, oozing, holes and weaker parts.</p>
<p>Bambu Lab recommends drying PETG before use; its material table gives 70 °C for 8 hours in a dryer, while Prusa dries its own PETG at 55 °C for 6 hours. See our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-dry-filament/">how to dry filament</a> for methods and a full temperature table, and store spools sealed with desiccant afterwards.</p>
<h2>PETG HF and other PETG variants</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>PETG HF</strong> – &quot;HF&quot; stands for high flow. Bambu Lab&#39;s PETG HF is made to be printed fast at high volumetric flow; its wiki gives separate &quot;Bambu PETG HF&quot; and &quot;Generic PETG HF&quot; profiles and warns against using the fast Bambu profile on damp filament. Other brands sell similar &quot;high-speed&quot; or &quot;rapid&quot; PETG.</li>
<li><strong>Carbon- or glass-fibre PETG (PETG-CF, PETG-GF)</strong> – stiffer and more matte. Bambu Lab marks PETG-CF as needing a hardened nozzle.</li>
<li><strong>Translucent and matte PETG</strong> – same base material with a different finish.</li>
<li><strong>Recycled PETG</strong> – made from reclaimed material; use the settings on that spool&#39;s label.</li>
</ul>
<p>The <a href="/blog/bambu-filament/">Bambu filament guide</a> covers how the Basic, HF and Translucent lines fit together if you print on a Bambu Lab machine.</p>
<h2>Checklist for your first PETG print</h2>
<ol>
<li>Dry the spool if it has been open for a while.</li>
<li>Load your slicer&#39;s PETG profile for your brand.</li>
<li>Use a textured sheet, or glue stick on smooth PEI.</li>
<li>Lower part cooling compared with PLA.</li>
<li>Print a small test part and look for strings and layer gaps.</li>
<li>Adjust temperature in 5 °C steps, then retraction, if needed.</li>
<li>Store the spool sealed with desiccant after printing.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>PETG filament is the practical step up from PLA: tougher, more heat-tolerant and still easy to print without an enclosure. Use it for anything that bends, gets warm or lives outdoors, accept a little stringing, keep the spool dry and protect smooth beds with glue stick.</p>
