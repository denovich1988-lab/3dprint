<h2>What PETG and ABS have in common</h2>
<p>Both materials sit one step above PLA. They are tougher, take more heat and bend before they break, so they suit brackets, clips, mounts, enclosures and printer parts rather than display models. Both absorb some moisture from the air and print better when dry. And both need a heated bed and a hot end that reaches at least 250 °C – a limit most current printers meet.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> if PLA parts crack, creep or sag in the heat, either material can be the fix. The choice is about what your printer can handle and what the part must survive.</p>
<h2>Heat resistance: ABS wins</h2>
<p>Polymaker gives PETG a glass transition of about 80–85 °C and puts ABS at about 100 °C. Prusa says PETG parts suit most exterior use as long as they stay below 80 °C, while ABS is the classic pick for parts near motors, lamps or in a hot car. Above the glass transition a plastic softens and slowly deforms under load, so a 20 °C gap matters for parts that carry weight while warm.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for a part that sits behind a car windscreen or next to a heater, choose ABS (or ASA). For a shelf bracket, a tool holder or a desk organiser, PETG has more than enough heat resistance. Our <a href="/blog/abs-filament/">ABS filament guide</a> covers the heat-related settings in more detail.</p>
<h2>Strength and toughness: closer than you think</h2>
<p>&quot;Is PETG stronger than ABS?&quot; has no single answer. Both are tough and flex instead of snapping. UltiMaker&#39;s comparison describes PETG as a strong all-rounder and ABS as tough and impact-resistant, and the exact figures vary from brand to brand. PETG&#39;s layer adhesion is usually very good, so printed parts are less likely to split along a layer. ABS can split between layers if the print cools unevenly, which is one more reason to use an enclosure.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if strength really matters, compare the technical data sheets of the two exact filaments you would buy and print a test part in each orientation. Differences between brands and between print settings are often bigger than the difference between PETG and ABS as materials.</p>
<h2>Ease of printing: PETG wins</h2>
<p>This is the biggest practical difference. PETG has very low thermal expansion, so flat parts stay down on the bed without an enclosure. ABS shrinks about 1–2 % as it cools; without a warm, enclosed chamber the corners lift and tall parts crack between layers. Prusa recommends an enclosure for ABS and says it is required for larger parts.</p>
<p>PETG has its own quirks. It strings more than PLA or ABS, bridges and overhangs are weaker, and it can bond so strongly to a smooth PEI sheet that it tears pieces out. Prusa advises a textured (powder-coated) sheet for PETG, or glue stick as a release layer on smooth PEI.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> on an open-frame printer, PETG is the practical choice. On an enclosed printer, both are easy, and the choice comes down to the part. See our <a href="/blog/petg-filament/">PETG filament guide</a> for stringing and adhesion fixes.</p>
<h2>Fumes and smell: PETG wins</h2>
<p>ABS releases styrene and other emissions when it is heated, and makers recommend printing it in a well-ventilated room or with filtration. PETG has little smell in comparison. If your printer sits in a bedroom, office or classroom, that alone often decides the question.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you do print ABS indoors, use an enclosed printer, keep the door closed until the part cools, and air the room afterwards. Many enclosed printers have a carbon filter or an exhaust fan; check that yours is fitted and switched on.</p>
<h2>Outdoor use and UV: neither is ideal</h2>
<p>Hubs and Creality describe PETG&#39;s UV resistance as moderate, so it lasts longer outdoors than PLA. ABS has poor UV resistance – the butadiene rubber in it breaks down in sunlight, so it yellows and turns brittle. For long-term sun exposure, makers point to ASA, which prints like ABS but resists UV.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for garden fittings and planters in partial shade, PETG is usually fine. For parts in full sun for years, use ASA. Our <a href="/blog/asa-vs-abs/">ASA vs ABS comparison</a> explains that choice.</p>
<h2>Finishing, gluing and smoothing: ABS wins</h2>
<p>ABS sands easily, takes paint well and dissolves in acetone. That means you can glue ABS parts with acetone or ABS slurry and smooth the surface with acetone vapour for a glossy finish. PETG resists most chemical smoothing, so finishing is mainly sanding, and gluing needs a suitable adhesive such as cyanoacrylate.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> for cosplay pieces, enclosures that must look moulded, or large parts printed in sections and glued together, ABS is easier to finish. For parts that just need to work, PETG&#39;s resistance to chemicals is an advantage, not a drawback.</p>

<h2>Checklist before you switch materials</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check your printer.</strong> ABS needs an enclosure; PETG does not. Both need a hot end that reaches about 250 °C.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the spool</strong> if it has been open for weeks – damp PETG strings, damp ABS bubbles.</li>
<li><strong>Load the right profile</strong> for the exact brand in your slicer.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the bed.</strong> Textured PEI for both; glue stick on smooth PEI for PETG.</li>
<li><strong>Set cooling.</strong> Moderate fan for PETG, low or off for ABS.</li>
<li><strong>Print a test part</strong> – a clip or bracket you actually need – and load it the way it will be used.</li>
<li><strong>Check the model first.</strong> Open the file in our <a href="/stl-viewer/">STL viewer</a> to check size and wall thickness before printing.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>If you print on an open-frame machine or in a living space, choose PETG: it is tough, easy and handles everyday heat. If you have an enclosed printer and the part will get hot, or you want a smooth, painted finish, choose ABS. For sun-exposed outdoor parts, skip both and use ASA.</p>
