<h2>What does &quot;strongest&quot; mean for a 3D printer filament?</h2>
<p>Data sheets list several properties, and each one answers a different question:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tensile strength (MPa)</strong> – how hard you can pull before the part breaks. This is the number most &quot;strongest filament&quot; lists quote.</li>
<li><strong>Stiffness (tensile or bending modulus, MPa)</strong> – how much the part bends under load. A stiff part can still be brittle. Bambu Lab lists PET-CF at 5,320 MPa bending modulus against 2,750 MPa for PLA Basic.</li>
<li><strong>Impact strength (kJ/m²)</strong> – how much energy the part absorbs before it cracks when hit or dropped. This is what people usually mean by &quot;tough&quot;.</li>
<li><strong>Layer adhesion (the Z values)</strong> – how well the layers stick together. Most real failures happen here.</li>
<li><strong>Heat deflection temperature (°C)</strong> – the temperature at which the part starts to sag under a small load. A strong PLA bracket in a hot car is no longer strong.</li>
<li><strong>Elongation at break (%)</strong> – how far the material stretches before it snaps. TPU 95A exceeds 700% in Bambu Lab&#39;s data sheet; PET-CF breaks at 4.5%.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> write down the load first – pull, bend, impact, heat, or a mix – and then pick the material that wins on that property. &quot;Strongest overall&quot; rarely exists.</p>
<h2>Which is the strongest 3D printer filament by property?</h2>
<p>Reading the table above by column gives a clearer answer than any single ranking:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Highest tensile strength along the layers:</strong> PA6-CF (102 MPa), then PAHT-CF (88 MPa) and PET-CF (74 MPa).</li>
<li><strong>Highest strength between layers:</strong> PAHT-CF (64 MPa), then PC (56 MPa) and PA6-CF (48 MPa). PET-CF drops from 74 MPa to 35 MPa across the layers – less than half.</li>
<li><strong>Toughest:</strong> TPU 95A by far (125.2 kJ/m² X-Y, 88.7 kJ/m² Z), then PAHT-CF (57.5) and PETG Basic (52.7).</li>
<li><strong>Stiffest:</strong> carbon-fibre grades. Bambu Lab&#39;s filament guide lists PA6-CF at 5,460 MPa and PET-CF at 5,320 MPa bending modulus, roughly double PLA.</li>
<li><strong>Most heat resistant:</strong> PET-CF (205 °C), PAHT-CF (194 °C), PA6-CF (186 °C). Of the unfilled materials, PC leads.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> if you ask &quot;what is the strongest 3D printer filament&quot; for a load-bearing hook, PAHT-CF or PC is a better answer than PA6-CF, because a hook printed upright is loaded across its layers, where PAHT-CF and PC keep most of their strength.</p>

<h2>PLA, PLA+ and Tough PLA: strong enough for many parts</h2>
<p>Standard PLA has decent tensile strength and is stiff, but it is brittle and softens at about 57 °C (Bambu Lab&#39;s PLA Basic heat deflection at 0.45 MPa). Bambu Lab&#39;s PLA Tough raises tensile strength to 44 MPa and impact strength to 31.2 kJ/m², with the same low heat resistance. Other brands sell similar modified grades as PLA+, PLA Pro or Tough PLA, with no common standard behind the names – see <a href="/blog/pla-vs-pla-plus/">PLA vs PLA+</a> for how they differ.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> PLA is a sensible choice for stiff indoor parts, jigs and prototypes. Do not use it for anything that sits in a car, in the sun or near a motor.</p>
<h2>PETG, ABS and ASA: the tough middle ground</h2>
<p>PETG Basic beats PLA on tensile strength (48 vs 39 MPa) and doubles its impact strength, which is why it is a common upgrade for functional parts; our <a href="/blog/petg-vs-pla/">PETG vs PLA</a> comparison covers the trade-offs in detail. ABS and ASA are not stronger than PETG in tension (41 and 37 MPa), but they resist heat better – 87 °C and 100 °C – and ASA adds UV resistance for outdoor parts. Both need an enclosure to stop warping and splitting. See <a href="/blog/asa-vs-abs/">ASA vs ABS</a> to choose between them.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> ABS and ASA have the weakest layer impact strength in the table. Prusa&#39;s troubleshooting guide notes that ABS is highly prone to layer separation when cold air reaches the print and recommends turning the part-cooling fan off and printing in an enclosure.</p>
<h2>Polycarbonate: the strongest unfilled filament</h2>
<p>PC is the strongest plain material in Bambu Lab&#39;s line-up: 62 MPa along the layers, 56 MPa across them, and a bending strength of 108 MPa. Its small loss in the Z direction makes it predictable for load-bearing parts, and its heat resistance (112–117 °C) is the highest of the unfilled grades. The catch is printing: Bambu Lab printed its test specimens at a 270 °C nozzle and 100 °C bed, and PC warps without a warm, enclosed chamber.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> choose PC when you need strength in every direction and heat resistance, and your printer is enclosed and reaches at least 270 °C at the nozzle.</p>
<h2>Nylon and carbon-fibre composites: the strongest on most home printers</h2>
<p>Nylon (PA6, PA12 and high-temperature blends) is tough and wear-resistant, and adding chopped carbon fibre makes it much stiffer and more heat resistant. That is why PA6-CF and PAHT-CF top the table for tensile strength and heat. Bambu Lab printed both at 280–290 °C with a 100 °C bed, and like all fibre-filled filaments they need a hardened nozzle. Our <a href="/blog/carbon-fiber-filament/">carbon fiber filament guide</a> covers nozzles, settings and safety.</p>
<p>Two cautions apply:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Moisture.</strong> Nylon absorbs water quickly. Bambu Lab lists saturated water absorption of 2.35% for PA6-CF and 0.88% for PAHT-CF, against 0.43% for PLA. Its data sheets give dry-state values only, so dry the spool before printing and store it sealed.</li>
<li><strong>Fibres trade toughness for stiffness.</strong> PETG-CF has lower unnotched impact strength than plain PETG Basic (41.2 vs 52.7 kJ/m²), and PET-CF is the most brittle material in the table between layers.</li>
</ul>
<p>Unfilled nylon is more flexible and impact-resistant than CF grades, but makers&#39; numbers vary widely by blend and moisture content, so check the specific spool&#39;s data sheet.</p>
<h2>TPU: the toughest, not the strongest</h2>
<p>TPU 95A has the lowest tensile strength in the table (29.6 MPa) but by far the highest impact strength, and it stretches more than 700% before breaking. Parts made from it bend instead of cracking, so for bumpers, drone guards, phone cases, gaskets and vibration mounts it outlasts every rigid filament. It is too soft for anything that must hold its shape under load. Our TPU filament guide covers print settings.</p>
<h2>PEEK, PEI (ULTEM) and other high-performance filaments</h2>
<p>PEEK, PEKK and PEI (sold under the ULTEM brand) are stronger and far more heat resistant than anything above, and they are also self-extinguishing and chemical resistant. They are not filaments for a normal desktop printer:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PEEK-CF:</strong> Prusa&#39;s knowledge base lists a 380–440 °C nozzle, a 140–155 °C bed and an actively heated chamber at 85 °C or more, plus drying at 130 °C for at least four hours.</li>
<li><strong>PEI / ULTEM:</strong> Prusa lists a 150–155 °C bed, an 85–90 °C chamber and drying at 130–150 °C, and warns about warping and difficult support removal. It claims heat resistance above 200 °C.</li>
</ul>
<p>Prusa recommends both for its Pro HT90 industrial printer. Other makers also sell high-temperature composites such as PPS-CF and PPA-CF for their high-end machines; check each printer&#39;s material list before buying a spool.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> if your printer tops out at 300 °C and has no heated chamber, the strongest filament you can print reliably is a CF nylon or PC, not PEEK.</p>
<h2>How to make 3D prints stronger: settings checklist</h2>
<p>The material sets the ceiling; the settings decide how close you get to it. Work through this list in order:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Orient for the load.</strong> Put the main stress along the layers, as shown above. This often matters more than switching material.</li>
<li><strong>Add walls before infill.</strong> Prusa&#39;s documentation says a model&#39;s strength is mostly defined by the number of perimeters, not the infill, and its profiles use at least two. For functional parts, try 4–6 walls and thicker top and bottom shells.</li>
<li><strong>Keep infill sensible.</strong> Prusa&#39;s infill guide calls 10–20% the best balance for most parts and says going above about 40% makes little sense; gyroid supports the part well in every direction. Use high infill only for parts under heavy compression.</li>
<li><strong>Print hot enough.</strong> Too low a nozzle temperature is a common cause of weak layers. Prusa suggests raising it in 5 °C steps within the maker&#39;s range until layers bond well, and raising the flow multiplier in 5% steps after calibration.</li>
<li><strong>Control cooling and draughts.</strong> Reduce or turn off the part fan for ABS and ASA, and use an enclosure for ABS, ASA, PC and nylon.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the filament.</strong> Wet nylon, PC and PETG print with bubbles and weaker layers. Dry them before printing and store them sealed.</li>
<li><strong>Anneal where the maker allows it.</strong> Bambu Lab annealed all its test specimens before testing, and its PET-CF and PAHT-CF data sheets suggest annealing printed parts at about 80–140 °C for several hours, warning that parts may warp. Test on a spare part first.</li>
<li><strong>Check the file.</strong> Thin walls in the model limit how many perimeters fit. Open it in a 3MF or STL viewer to look for thin sections and check the size before slicing.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Verdict</h2>
