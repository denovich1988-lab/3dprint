<h2>What is TPU filament made of</h2>
<p>TPU is a polymer with hard and soft segments. The soft parts let it stretch; the hard parts hold it together and make it tough. That mix gives a material that behaves somewhere between rubber and plastic.</p>
<p>Prusa describes TPU as a subtype of TPE, with the main difference being hardness.</p>
<h2>Shore hardness: 95A, 85A and softer</h2>
<p>TPU is graded on the <strong>Shore A scale</strong>. A higher number is harder:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>95A–98A</strong> – firm but flexible, like a phone case. The easiest TPU to print and the best starting point.</li>
<li><strong>85A–90A</strong> – noticeably softer, like a shoe sole.</li>
<li><strong>Below 85A</strong> – very soft and stretchy, and much harder to feed through an extruder.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> If this is your first flexible filament, buy a 95A grade. Soft grades are where most feeding problems happen.</p>
<h2>How to print TPU</h2>
<p>Flexible filament is soft, so the extruder can push it sideways instead of forward. Most of the rules exist to stop that.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Print slowly.</strong> Lower speeds give the extruder time to push the soft strand without buckling. Start with your slicer&#39;s TPU profile, which is already slower than PLA.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the path short and enclosed.</strong> Direct-drive extruders handle TPU better than Bowden setups. Avoid gaps where the filament can escape between gears and tubes.</li>
<li><strong>Skip the multi-material feeder.</strong> Bambu Lab notes that its AMS does not support filaments that are too soft. Load TPU directly from an external spool holder.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce or turn off retraction.</strong> Long, fast retractions stretch and jam soft filament.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the spool.</strong> Prusa warns that flexible filaments absorb moisture and become hard to print when wet.</li>
<li><strong>Watch bed adhesion.</strong> TPU can stick too well. Prusa suggests glue stick as a release layer on smooth PEI, or a textured sheet.</li>
</ol>
<h2>What to print with TPU</h2>
<ul>
<li>Phone, tablet and camera cases</li>
<li>Gaskets, seals and washers</li>
<li>Bumpers and corner protectors</li>
<li>RC car tyres and robot wheels</li>
<li>Watch straps and cable ties</li>
<li>Anti-vibration feet for printers and appliances</li>
</ul>
<p>Prusa lists stamps, RC tyres, shoe soles and belts as typical flexible-filament projects.</p>
<h2>TPU vs PLA and PETG</h2>
<p>TPU is not a replacement for PLA or PETG. Use it when a part must flex, absorb impact or grip. For rigid parts, PLA is easier and PETG is tougher; see our <a href="/blog/petg-vs-pla/">PETG vs PLA comparison</a>.</p>
