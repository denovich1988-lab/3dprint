Facts come from the material and printer makers – Victrex, 3DXTech, 3D4Makers, INTAMSYS, Evonik, Prusa, Stratasys, Bambu Lab and QIDI – checked November 2026. We have not printed PEEK ourselves and we do not compare prices.</p>

<h2>What is PEEK? The PAEK family explained</h2>
<p>PEEK stands for polyether ether ketone. Victrex, the company that commercialised it, describes it as part of the aromatic polyketone family, more precisely called polyaryletherketones (PAEK). ICI filed the PEEK patent in 1978, and Victrex brought it to market in 1981.</p>
<p>The chain is built from three units: rigid aryl rings and ketone groups, which give stiffness and a high melting point, and ether links, which add flexibility and toughness. Because the repeat unit is regular, PEEK can partly crystallise. Victrex credits that crystalline structure for much of PEEK&#39;s wear, creep, fatigue and chemical resistance.</p>
<p>Other members of the PAEK family you will see on filament shelves:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PEKK</strong> – another polyketone with a different ether/ketone ratio, sold for example in 3DXTech&#39;s ThermaX range and as PEKK-CF for the Prusa Pro HT90.</li>
<li><strong>PAEK and LMPAEK</strong> – lower-melting grades. Victrex&#39;s AM 200 filament is a PAEK with a melting point of 303 °C (vs 343 °C for PEEK), and Victrex claims better printed-part strength and printability than PEEK on most machines.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> &quot;PEEK&quot;, &quot;PEKK&quot; and &quot;PAEK&quot; are not interchangeable on a spool label. They need different temperatures and annealing cycles, so always print from the datasheet for the exact grade.</p>
<h2>PEEK properties from material datasheets</h2>
<p>Victrex lists these values for its PEEK polymer (moulded material): a glass transition of 143 °C, a melting point of 343 °C, a heat deflection temperature of 152 °C and a continuous use temperature of 260 °C. Its range of tensile strengths runs from 78 MPa for unfilled grades to 330 MPa for fibre-reinforced ones. Victrex also says PEEK resists hydrocarbons, organic solvents and strong bases, is not attacked by water or steam, resists autoignition up to almost 600 °C and gives off little smoke.</p>

<h2>PEEK filament types: unfilled, carbon fibre, glass fibre and implant grade</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Unfilled (natural) PEEK</strong> – the base material, for example 3DXTech ThermaX PEEK, INTAMSYS PEEK and 3D4Makers PEEK.</li>
<li><strong>PEEK-CF</strong> – carbon-fibre-filled PEEK such as 3DXTech CarbonX PEEK+CF10. Prusa lists low weight, high dimensional stability and stiffness as the gains.</li>
<li><strong>PEEK-GF</strong> – glass-fibre-filled PEEK such as 3DXTech Fibrex PEEK+GF20, also sold by INTAMSYS.</li>
<li><strong>Implant-grade PEEK</strong> – Evonik sells VESTAKEEP i4 3DF, made to meet ASTM F2026 (the standard for PEEK used in surgical implants), plus carbon-fibre grades with 12% and 20% fibre (iC4612 and iC4620), an osteoconductive grade (iC4800) and a purple test grade (i4 3DF-T) with the same processing behaviour but without the documentation needed for medical approval.</li>
</ul>
<p>3DXTech says its carbon and glass fibre PEEK grades need a hardened steel nozzle. Fibres wear a standard nozzle quickly, as they do with carbon fiber filaments for desktop printers.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> PEEK melts at 343 °C, so a hot end must hold well above that – around 400 °C – just to extrude it at a usable viscosity. A hot end that peaks at 350 °C is barely past the melting point.</p>
<h2>Annealing PEEK and crystallinity</h2>
<p>PEEK&#39;s properties depend on how much of it crystallises. When hot PEEK is laid down on a cooler part, it freezes before the chains can line up, so the print comes out largely <strong>amorphous</strong>. 3D4Makers says prints are made in the amorphous phase, which reduces shrinkage, and recommends annealing afterwards to reach the semi-crystalline phase. INTAMSYS notes that heating, deposition, cooling and post-processing all affect crystallinity and that the right strategy depends on grade, geometry and the properties you need.</p>

<h2>Why hobby printers can&#39;t print PEEK</h2>
<p>Compare the requirements with high-end desktop printers:</p>
<ul>
<li>The <a href="/printers/bambu-lab-h2d/">Bambu Lab H2D</a> has a 350 °C hot end and a chamber heated up to 65 °C, according to Bambu Lab&#39;s launch post.</li>
<li>The QIDI Plus 4 lists a 370 °C nozzle, a 120 °C bed and a 65 °C chamber.</li>
<li>Most open and enclosed desktop printers stop at 300 °C or below.</li>
</ul>
<p>Even the best of these miss at least two targets: PEEK makers ask for about 400 °C at the nozzle, 120–155 °C at the bed and 70 °C or more in the chamber. Without a hot chamber, the first layers cool and shrink while new layers go down hot, which leads to warping, cracks and weak layer bonds. Never set a hot end above the temperature its maker rates it for. 3DXTech states plainly that its PEEK is not intended for standard desktop FDM printers, and Prusa says its PEI 1010 is not suitable for the MK, XL and MINI series.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> if your printer tops out at 300–350 °C, choose PC, PAHT-CF, PA6-CF or PET-CF for heat-resistant parts instead of a PEEK spool that will not print properly.</p>

<h2>Troubleshooting PEEK prints</h2>
<p><strong>Warping and cracking between layers</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Causes: chamber too cold, bed too cold, large flat parts, draughts.</li>
<li>Fixes in order: raise the chamber towards the top of the maker&#39;s range; raise the bed within the range; add a brim; slow down.</li>
<li>Prevention: Prusa notes that heating the chamber reduces warping and cracking and improves layer adhesion.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>First layer won&#39;t stick</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Causes: no adhesive, bed under temperature, dirty surface.</li>
<li>Fixes in order: clean the plate; apply a high-temperature adhesive (3DXTech uses Nano Polymer Adhesive, Prusa and 3D4Makers suggest Magigoo HT, applied in several layers); confirm bed temperature.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Bubbles, popping, rough or weak parts</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cause: moisture. Prusa says PEEK-CF starts absorbing moisture within 10 minutes in open air.</li>
<li>Fixes in order: dry at 120–130 °C for the time on the datasheet; print from a heated dry box; reprint.</li>
<li>Prevention: check that your dryer and spool are rated for 120 °C or more – many hobby dryers are not. Our <a href="/blog/filament-dryer/">filament dryer guide</a> covers typical limits.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Part changes size after annealing</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cause: crystallisation shrinkage.</li>
<li>Fixes: anneal a test part first, measure, and scale the model or adjust tolerances.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Checklist: before you buy PEEK filament</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check the printer&#39;s real limits:</strong> hot end of about 400 °C or more, bed of 120 °C or more, actively heated chamber of 70 °C or more.</li>
<li><strong>Pick the exact grade</strong> – unfilled, CF, GF or PAEK – and download its datasheet.</li>
<li><strong>Fit a hardened nozzle</strong> for CF and GF grades.</li>
<li><strong>Plan drying:</strong> a dryer that reaches 120–130 °C and a heated dry box for printing.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare bed adhesion:</strong> the adhesive or sheet the maker names.</li>
<li><strong>Plan annealing:</strong> an oven that holds the datasheet temperature steadily, and a test part to measure shrinkage.</li>
<li><strong>Plan the room:</strong> high-temperature printers and annealing ovens give off a lot of heat, so keep the space ventilated and follow the printer maker&#39;s installation guidance.</li>
<li><strong>Check the model first.</strong> Open it in our <a href="/stl-viewer/">STL viewer</a> to confirm scale and wall thickness before you spend expensive filament.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>PEEK filament gives FDM printing its highest-performance plastic: a 343 °C melting point, up to 260 °C continuous use, strong chemical and wear resistance and V-0 flame behaviour. The price of that is a demanding process – about 400 °C at the nozzle, a 120–155 °C bed, a hot chamber, dry filament and usually an annealing step. That puts it on industrial machines such as the INTAMSYS FUNMAT series or the Prusa Pro HT90, not on desktop printers that stop at 300–350 °C. If you need heat resistance on a home printer, PC or a carbon fibre nylon is the realistic choice; if you need flame-rated parts without crystallisation, look at PEI.</p>
