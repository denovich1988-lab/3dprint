<h2>What a cheap 3D printer usually has</h2>
<p>Nearly every entry-level FDM printer shares a few traits:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Open frame.</strong> No enclosure, which keeps the machine light and simple. Of the models above, only the Flashforge Adventurer 3 is enclosed.</li>
<li><strong>A bed-slinger design.</strong> The bed moves front to back on the Y axis. It is a simple, proven layout; the trade-offs are extra depth on the desk and less speed at high accelerations. The Creality K1 SE (CoreXY) and Ender 3 V3 (CoreXZ) are exceptions.</li>
<li><strong>A standard bed</strong> of about 220 to 256 mm, or 180 mm on compact models such as the A1 mini and Prusa MINI+.</li>
<li><strong>A heated bed</strong> reaching 80 to 110 °C – enough for PLA, PETG and TPU.</li>
<li><strong>A 0.4 mm nozzle</strong> for 1.75 mm filament.</li>
</ul>
<p>Newer entry-level models add things that used to be premium: automatic bed levelling (CR Touch on the Ender 3 V3 SE, LeviQ on Anycubic printers, an inductive probe on the Neptune 3 Pro), direct-drive extruders that handle flexible TPU, input shaping for faster printing and Klipper firmware on the Neptune 4.</p>
<h2>What budget printers usually leave out</h2>
<p>The savings come from somewhere. Features most often missing or sold as extras:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Enclosure.</strong> Without one, ABS and ASA are hard to print because parts warp and crack as they cool unevenly. See our <a href="/blog/abs-filament/">ABS filament</a> guide for what that material needs.</li>
<li><strong>Network and app control.</strong> The Ender 3 V3 SE, Neptune 3 Pro, original Kobra and original Ender-3 print from an SD card or USB only.</li>
<li><strong>Camera.</strong> Built in on the Creality Hi and the Adventurer 3; optional on the K1 SE, Ender 3 V3 and Kobra 3; absent on most others.</li>
<li><strong>Multi-colour.</strong> Usually an add-on – the AMS lite for the Bambu Lab A1 and A1 mini, ACE Pro for the Kobra 3, CFS for the Creality Hi. &quot;Combo&quot; versions include the unit.</li>
<li><strong>Sensors.</strong> A filament runout sensor and power-loss recovery are common but not universal; the original Ender-3, for example, has power-loss resume but no runout sensor, according to Creality.</li>
<li><strong>High temperatures.</strong> Hotends on the older or simpler models above top out at 240 to 260 °C, which limits nylon and some engineering filaments.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> decide which of these you really need. If you print PLA toys and household parts at home, an enclosure and camera add little. If you want to print while away, network control matters more than speed.</p>
<h2>Build volume: compact or standard?</h2>
<p>A compact printer (about 180 mm) is smaller, lighter and easier to fit on a desk, and suits figures, keychains, small tools and classroom work. A standard bed (220 to 256 mm) fits most models people download and leaves room to print several parts at once.</p>
<p>Height is usually less important than bed size, because tall parts can often be laid down or split. If you regularly print helmets, cosplay pieces or long brackets, an entry-level printer will be too small; our <a href="/blog/large-3d-printer/">large 3D printer</a> guide covers the next size up. Before buying, open a few of the files you want to print in our <a href="/stl-viewer/">STL viewer</a> to see their dimensions.</p>
<h2>Extruder: direct drive or Bowden</h2>
<p>A <strong>direct-drive</strong> extruder sits on the toolhead and pushes filament a short distance into the hotend. It handles flexible TPU and quick retractions well, and it is now standard on most budget printers, from the Ender 3 V3 SE to the Neptune 4. A <strong>Bowden</strong> extruder sits on the frame and pushes filament through a long tube; it keeps the toolhead light but makes soft filaments harder to feed. The original Ender-3 and the Prusa MINI+ use Bowden extruders.</p>
<h2>Bed levelling: manual or automatic</h2>
<p>Manual levelling – the paper test at each corner – works, but it is the step beginners struggle with most, and an uneven first layer causes most early failures. Automatic levelling probes the bed and builds a mesh, and some printers also set the Z offset automatically (the Ender 3 V3 SE uses a strain sensor for this). For a first printer, automatic levelling is the single feature most worth having.</p>
<h2>Speed claims on budget printers</h2>
<p>Makers quote top speeds of 250 to 600 mm/s for the newer models in the table. These are maximum movement speeds; real print speed depends on the filament, the hotend&#39;s melting capacity, cooling and the part. Anycubic, for example, lists the Kobra 3 at 600 mm/s maximum with 300 mm/s recommended. Treat speed as a secondary factor behind reliability and ease of use.</p>
<h2>Materials a budget printer handles</h2>
<p>With an open frame and a bed up to 100 or 110 °C, entry-level printers are made for:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PLA</strong> – the easiest material and the default for beginners. See our <a href="/blog/pla-filament/">PLA filament</a> guide.</li>
<li><strong>PETG</strong> – tougher and more heat-resistant than PLA. Our <a href="/blog/petg-vs-pla/">PETG vs PLA</a> comparison explains the differences.</li>
<li><strong>TPU</strong> – flexible parts, best on a direct-drive extruder.</li>
</ul>
<p>ABS and ASA are possible on some of them but need care and ideally an enclosure. Abrasive filaments such as carbon-fibre blends need a hardened nozzle; check whether your printer offers one.</p>
<h2>Cheap resin printers</h2>
<p>Entry-level resin (MSLA) printers are a different category. They print very fine detail – the Elegoo Mars 3 lists 0.035 mm XY resolution – but the build area is small (143 × 89.6 × 175 mm on the Mars 3, 130 × 80 × 165 mm on the Anycubic Photon Mono). They also need liquid resin, gloves, ventilation, washing and curing. Choose resin for miniatures and jewellery models; choose FDM for larger, functional parts.</p>
<h2>The real cost of owning a cheap 3D printer</h2>
<p>The purchase price is only part of the cost. Plan for:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Filament</strong> – a spool or two to start, and a dry box if you live somewhere humid.</li>
<li><strong>Spare nozzles and wear parts</strong> – nozzles wear and clog; our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-clean-a-3d-printer-nozzle/">how to clean a 3D printer nozzle</a> explains how to clear one, but spares are worth having.</li>
<li><strong>Build plates</strong> – textured and smooth PEI plates wear over time.</li>
<li><strong>Your time</strong> – a printer with manual levelling or a kit build takes longer to set up and tune.</li>
</ul>
<p>Printers from makers with good documentation, slicer profiles and easy-to-find spare parts usually cost less over time.</p>
<h2>Checklist: what to check before buying a cheap 3D printer</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Measure what you want to print.</strong> Make sure the bed fits your largest typical part.</li>
<li><strong>Choose your materials.</strong> PLA, PETG and TPU work on any printer in the table; ABS and ASA want an enclosure.</li>
<li><strong>Insist on automatic bed levelling</strong> if this is your first printer.</li>
<li><strong>Prefer a direct-drive extruder</strong> if you want to print TPU.</li>
<li><strong>Check connectivity.</strong> SD card only, LAN or Wi-Fi with an app – decide what you need.</li>
<li><strong>Check sensors.</strong> Filament runout detection and power-loss recovery save long prints.</li>
<li><strong>Check the maximum temperatures</strong> against the filaments you plan to use.</li>
<li><strong>Check the space.</strong> Bed-slingers need room front and back for the bed to move.</li>
<li><strong>Check support.</strong> Slicer profiles, firmware updates, an official manual and spare parts.</li>
<li><strong>Compare the current price</strong> across a few sellers yourself – prices change too often to quote here.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>A cheap 3D printer is a good way into 3D printing as long as it matches what you will print. For most beginners, the best value comes from an open-frame printer with automatic levelling and a direct-drive extruder – models such as the Bambu Lab A1 mini or A1, Creality Ender 3 V3 SE, Elegoo Neptune 4 or Prusa MINI+ fit that description. Choose a compact model if space is tight, a standard 220 to 256 mm bed if you want more room, and a printer with network control if you want to start and watch prints remotely. If you mainly need ABS, ASA or very large parts, a budget open-frame printer is the wrong tool. Compare the specs of your shortlist side by side with our <a href="/printers/compare/">comparison tool</a> before you decide.</p>
