<h2>Articulated 3D printed animals</h2>
<p>Flexi animals are print-in-place models whose segments are separated by small gaps, so the body bends as soon as it leaves the plate. Common subjects include axolotls, sharks, cats, snakes, octopuses, lizards and dinosaurs. Many octopus and lizard designs are made to print with no supports and no assembly; some long snakes stretch well past the size of the bed they were printed on because they print coiled up.</p>
<p>The rules are the same as for any print-in-place design: no supports, layers of 0.2 mm or thinner, elephant foot compensation on and print at the designed scale. We cover joint gaps and fused segments in detail in our <a href="/blog/3d-printed-dragon/">3D printed dragon</a> guide, and pocket-sized flexi creatures overlap with <a href="/blog/3d-printed-fidget-toys/">3D printed fidget toys</a>, so this guide focuses on the other types.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for tiny &quot;mini&quot; flexi animals, use a 0.12–0.16 mm layer height and a slower outer wall speed. The gaps between segments are the same size as on bigger models, but the walls around them are thinner.</p>
<h2>Low-poly animals</h2>
<p>Low-poly models build an animal from flat triangular faces, like a folded paper sculpture. They are easy to print because most faces slope gently enough to need no support, and there are no moving parts, so you can scale them up or down freely. Large low-poly heads, foxes, bears and deer make good shelf pieces.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> silk or metallic PLA catches the light differently on each facet, which suits low-poly shapes. Matte PLA gives a clean, modern look. Use 3 walls so the flat faces stay crisp and do not show infill through the surface.</p>
<h2>Realistic animal figurines</h2>
<p>Detailed figurines of dogs, horses, birds or wildlife need more care. Legs, tails, ears, horns and open mouths create overhangs, and fine surface detail needs thin layers.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Orientation:</strong> stand the animal on its feet or belly so the largest flat area sits on the bed and the face points up or sideways, where support marks matter least.</li>
<li><strong>Layer height:</strong> 0.08–0.12 mm brings out fur and feather detail; 0.16 mm is a common compromise for larger models.</li>
<li><strong>Supports:</strong> the Bambu Lab Wiki recommends tree or hybrid supports for objects with complex shapes where most overhangs are small, curved surfaces, because they hold well with less material and print time. Normal supports give a better finish under large flat overhangs.</li>
<li><strong>Support gap:</strong> when supports are printed in the same filament as the model, the Bambu Lab Wiki advises a top Z distance of about 0.2 mm, otherwise the supports are very hard to remove. A dedicated support material lets you use 0.</li>
<li><strong>Overhang threshold:</strong> Bambu Studio&#39;s default threshold angle is 30 degrees; raising it adds more support, which can help with thin legs and chins.</li>
</ul>
<p>If you need to add or block supports in particular spots, see our guide to <a href="/blog/bambu-studio-manual-supports/">Bambu Studio manual supports</a>.</p>
<h2>Animal planters and functional pieces</h2>
<p>Animal planters, pen pots, phone stands and toothbrush holders turn a cute model into something useful. These are usually printed upright with no or few supports.</p>
<ul>
<li>Use 3–4 walls and 4 or more bottom layers so water does not seep through.</li>
<li>For planters that hold damp soil or sit in sun outdoors, PETG is a better choice than PLA; see PETG vs PLA.</li>
<li>Choose a design with a drainage hole, or use the printed pot as a cover around a plastic nursery pot.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Multicolour 3D printed animals</h2>
<p>There are four ways to add colour, from simplest to most involved:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Gradient or multicolour filament.</strong> One spool changes colour along its length, which gives flexi animals a rainbow body with no extra work.</li>
<li><strong>Colour change at a layer.</strong> Most slicers let you add a filament change at a chosen layer. The Prusa Knowledge Base explains that the printer pauses when it reads the M600 command in the G-code and asks you to swap filament. This works well for animals with horizontal bands, such as a bee, a clownfish or a white-bellied penguin.</li>
<li><strong>Multi-material printing.</strong> A unit such as the Bambu Lab AMS swaps filaments automatically, so you can paint eyes, spots and stripes onto the model in the slicer. Many animal models come as ready-coloured 3MF projects for these systems.</li>
<li><strong>Painting</strong> after printing (see below).</li>
</ol>
<p>Multi-material prints purge filament at every colour change, so small details on many layers add waste and time. Check the estimate in the slicer before printing.</p>
<h2>Painting 3D printed animals</h2>
<p>Paint gives the most control over eyes, noses and markings. All3DP&#39;s painting guide recommends this order for PLA:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Clean up:</strong> trim support stubs with a craft knife or flush cutters.</li>
<li><strong>Sand:</strong> start around 120–220 grit to remove layer lines, then move up through finer grits. Go gently on small details such as ears and whiskers.</li>
<li><strong>Prime:</strong> primer is essential on PLA because paint does not grip it well. Two light coats of spray primer give even coverage; filler primer hides fine layer lines.</li>
<li><strong>Paint:</strong> acrylics are the easiest for beginners because they dry fast and clean up with water. Build colour in thin coats, and use fine brushes or water-based acrylic paint markers for eyes and details.</li>
<li><strong>Seal:</strong> a clear coat protects the paint and sets the sheen.</li>
</ol>
<p>Avoid paints that use acetone as the main solvent, as it can damage PLA.</p>
<h2>How to print your first 3D printed animal</h2>
<ol>
<li>Pick a print-in-place flexi animal or a low-poly model marked &quot;no supports&quot;.</li>
<li>Open the file in our <a href="/3d-viewer/">3D viewer</a> or STL viewer to check its size and that it is a single, closed model.</li>
<li>Slice in PLA at 0.2 mm with supports off, or follow the designer&#39;s notes.</li>
<li>Print the first layer slowly and watch it go down.</li>
<li>Let the plate cool, then remove the animal; flex any joints gently from the tail end.</li>
<li>When that works, move on to a figurine with tree supports and thinner layers.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Where to find animal models</h2>
<p>Printables, MakerWorld and Thingiverse all have large animal categories; search for terms such as &quot;flexi&quot;, &quot;articulated&quot;, &quot;low poly&quot; or &quot;planter&quot; plus the animal. Check the licence before selling prints. To turn a photo of your own pet into a printable keepsake, our <a href="/lithophane-maker/">lithophane maker</a> creates a backlit panel from an image right in your browser. For more ideas, see what to 3D print.</p>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>3D printed animals cover every level. Start with a flexi animal or a low-poly sculpture that needs no supports, then try a realistic figurine with tree supports and 0.12 mm layers. Use gradient filament, a layer colour change or a multi-material unit for colour, or prime and paint the print for the most detail.</p>
