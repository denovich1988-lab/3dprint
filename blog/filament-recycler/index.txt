This guide explains each step of the process, the kinds of systems available, which plastics recycle well, what results to expect and how to cut waste even without a recycler. Product facts come from the makers&#39; own pages and independent coverage, checked November 2026; prices are not compared.</p>

<h2>What a filament recycler is</h2>
<p>&quot;Filament recycler&quot; can mean one machine or a full line. Most systems combine three functions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A shredder</strong> turns parts and purge into flakes small enough to feed an extruder.</li>
<li><strong>An extruder</strong> – a heated barrel with a rotating screw – melts the flakes and pushes the molten plastic through a nozzle.</li>
<li><strong>A puller and winder</strong> draw the strand at a controlled speed (which sets its diameter), cool it and spool it neatly. Better machines measure the diameter continuously and adjust the puller speed.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> the shredder is the part people forget. A filament extruder alone can only use pellets or small flakes, so whole failed prints need shredding first.</p>
<h2>Types of filament recyclers</h2>
<p><strong>Open-source and DIY machines.</strong> Projects such as Precious Plastic publish plans for shredders and extruders aimed at community workshops, and academic projects such as the RepRapable Recyclebot describe printable, open-hardware filament extruders. These are cheap to build but need mechanical skill and tuning.</p>
<p><strong>Desktop extruders for makers.</strong> Companies such as Filabot and Felfil sell filament extruders and related equipment for small workshops, schools and labs. They are designed to make filament from pellets or recycled flakes.</p>
<p><strong>Consumer recycling systems.</strong> In 2026 Creality announced the Filament Maker M1 and Shredder R1, a desktop pair launched through crowdfunding and aimed at home users with failed prints and purge waste. Coverage of the launch describes it as one of the first attempts to make filament recycling a plug-and-play product for hobbyists.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> whatever system you consider, check which plastics it is designed for, whether it includes a shredder, and whether it measures diameter automatically.</p>
<h2>Which plastics can you recycle into filament?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>PLA</strong> – the most common waste and the most common recycling target. It melts at relatively low temperatures but degrades with each heat cycle and absorbs moisture.</li>
<li><strong>PETG</strong> – recycles well if dried thoroughly. It is tougher and can produce good recycled filament for functional parts.</li>
<li><strong>ABS and ASA</strong> – can be recycled but produce fumes when melted, so ventilation matters even more.</li>
<li><strong>TPU and other flexibles</strong> – hard to shred and feed; usually not a beginner material for recycling.</li>
<li><strong>Filled filaments</strong> (carbon fibre, glass fibre, wood, metal) – abrasive or unpredictable; avoid them unless the machine is designed for them.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> never mix plastics. PLA, PETG and ABS process at different temperatures and do not blend into a usable material. Keep separate bins and label them clearly.</p>
<h2>Why sorting and drying matter so much</h2>
<p>Most bad recycled filament comes from bad input:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mixed plastics</strong> create weak spots and blobs.</li>
<li><strong>Mixed colours</strong> turn into a murky brown or grey. That is fine for functional parts, but if you want colour, sort by colour too – or recycle mostly one colour and add masterbatch or fresh coloured pellets.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture</strong> turns into steam in the extruder, causing bubbles, a rough surface and diameter swings. Dry the flakes before extruding, just as you would dry a spool – our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-dry-filament/">how to dry filament</a> covers temperatures for common plastics.</li>
<li><strong>Dirt, glue stick and labels</strong> contaminate the melt. Wipe parts and remove stickers and tape.</li>
</ul>
<h2>What to expect from recycled filament</h2>
<p>Recycled filament can print well, but be realistic:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Strength drops with each melt cycle.</strong> Heating breaks polymer chains, so filament recycled several times is weaker and more brittle. Many users mix recycled flakes with fresh pellets to keep properties stable.</li>
<li><strong>Diameter tolerance is harder to hold.</strong> Commercial filament is made on industrial lines with tight control. A desktop line needs careful tuning to stay close to 1.75 mm, and variations show up as uneven extrusion.</li>
<li><strong>Colour is limited</strong> unless you sort carefully.</li>
<li><strong>There is a learning curve.</strong> Expect to scrap some filament while you dial in temperature, screw speed and pull speed.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> use your first recycled spools for prototypes, jigs, test prints and hidden parts, and print a calibration cube and a flow test before trusting them on long jobs.</p>
<h2>Where your waste comes from – and how to cut it first</h2>
<p>Before buying a recycler, reduce the waste you make:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Multi-colour purge.</strong> Automatic filament systems purge plastic at every colour change. Our guide to <a href="/blog/what-is-ams-in-3d-printing/">what AMS means in 3D printing</a> explains why. Slicers can flush into infill or into a sacrificial object, and grouping colours by layer reduces the number of changes.</li>
<li><strong>Supports.</strong> Orient parts to need fewer supports, use tree supports, and split models where it helps.</li>
<li><strong>Failed prints.</strong> Calibrate first layers, keep filament dry, and use a first-layer check if your printer has one.</li>
<li><strong>Test prints.</strong> Print calibration models small and at low infill.</li>
</ol>
<h2>The home recycling workflow, step by step</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Set up bins</strong> for each plastic type and keep supports, purge and failed prints in them.</li>
<li><strong>Clean</strong> the waste: remove glue, tape, labels and metal inserts.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-dry</strong> larger parts if they have been stored in a humid room.</li>
<li><strong>Shred</strong> to small, even flakes; sieve out dust and oversized pieces.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the flakes</strong> thoroughly before extruding.</li>
<li><strong>Extrude</strong> at the temperature and screw speed recommended for that plastic and machine; let it stabilise before keeping filament.</li>
<li><strong>Measure</strong> the diameter at intervals (or rely on the machine&#39;s sensor) and adjust the pull speed.</li>
<li><strong>Wind</strong> onto a clean spool, label it with plastic type, source and date, and store it in a sealed bag with desiccant.</li>
<li><strong>Test</strong> with a calibration print before using it for real parts.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Safety</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Shredders are dangerous.</strong> Keep hands and loose clothing away from the blades, use the guard and never clear a jam with the power on.</li>
<li><strong>Extruders are hot</strong> and can burn; let them cool before cleaning.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate.</strong> Melting plastic gives off fumes, especially ABS and ASA. Run the extruder in a ventilated space.</li>
<li><strong>Follow the maker&#39;s manual</strong> for the materials and temperatures your machine supports.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Alternatives to owning a filament recycler</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Buy recycled filament.</strong> Several filament makers sell recycled PLA and PETG made on industrial lines with consistent diameter.</li>
<li><strong>Use take-back schemes</strong> where a filament maker or local makerspace collects waste.</li>
<li><strong>Share a recycler</strong> in a makerspace, school or club, where the waste volume justifies the setup time.</li>
<li><strong>Reuse scraps creatively</strong> – offcuts and purge are handy for testing glues, paints and sanding.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>A filament recycler is worth it for makers who produce a steady stream of clean, sorted waste and enjoy tuning machines – schools, makerspaces, print farms and heavy multi-colour users. For most hobbyists, the bigger win is cutting waste in the slicer and buying recycled filament when it suits the job. If you do buy a recycler, sort and dry your waste carefully, blend in fresh material and keep recycled spools for parts where a small loss of strength does not matter. For more on materials, see our <a href="/blog/pla-filament/">PLA filament guide</a>.</p>
