<h2>PA6 vs PA12: which should you choose?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>PA6</strong> is the stronger, stiffer and more heat-resistant option, but it is the most moisture-sensitive and warps more. It suits stiff structural parts printed in an enclosure.</li>
<li><strong>PA12</strong> absorbs far less water, warps less and keeps its dimensions better in humid air. It is more flexible and impact resistant, and it handles chemicals well. It suits parts that must hold tolerances or live outside a dry room.</li>
</ul>
<p>Bambu Lab&#39;s own carbon fibre nylons show the difference clearly. Its PA6-based <strong>PA6-CF</strong> absorbs 2.35 % water at saturation (25 °C, 55 % RH), while <strong>PAHT-CF</strong>, which its data sheet lists as PA12 and other long-chain PA plus carbon fibre, absorbs 0.88 %. PA6-CF is higher in tensile strength along the layers (102 MPa against 88 MPa), but PAHT-CF holds more strength across the layers (64 MPa against 48 MPa) and has better impact strength (57.5 against 40.3 kJ/m²) and a higher heat deflection temperature (194 °C against 186 °C at 0.45 MPa). Both data sheets give dry, annealed values.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for a first nylon print, a PA12 or a carbon fibre nylon is more forgiving than plain PA6. Move to PA6 when you need its stiffness and can keep the filament bone dry.</p>
<h2>Is nylon filament strong?</h2>
<p>Yes – nylon is one of the strongest and toughest materials most home printers can handle, but its strength depends on the grade, the print orientation and how wet it is.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Unfilled nylon.</strong> Polymaker gives tensile strength up to 80 MPa with 15–30 % elongation at break. That high elongation is the key point: nylon bends and yields rather than snapping, which is why it works for clips, hinges and living-hinge-style parts.</li>
<li><strong>Carbon fibre nylon.</strong> Fibres trade some toughness for stiffness and heat resistance. Bambu Lab lists PA6-CF at a heat deflection temperature of 164 °C at 1.8 MPa; Polymaker&#39;s PolyMide PA6-CF lists 173 °C.</li>
<li><strong>Layer direction.</strong> Parts are weaker across their layers (PA6-CF: 102 MPa along, 48 MPa across), so orient the main load along the layers.</li>
<li><strong>Moisture.</strong> Makers publish dry-state figures; wet nylon is softer and less stiff.</li>
</ul>
<p>For a wider comparison with PC, PETG-CF, PET-CF and TPU, see our guide to the <a href="/blog/strongest-3d-printer-filament/">strongest 3D printer filament</a>.</p>

<p>A few points stand out:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The range is wide</strong> – from 230 °C (eSUN ePA) to 300 °C (PolyMide PA6-CF) – so a generic nylon profile is only a rough start.</li>
<li><strong>Low-warp grades break the rules.</strong> Polymaker&#39;s PA6-CF runs on a 25–50 °C bed; do not copy that to another brand.</li>
<li><strong>Cooling is low or off.</strong> Bambu Lab lists 0–60 % fan for PA6-CF and Polymaker says fan off for PolyMide PA6-CF.</li>
<li><strong>Nozzle size.</strong> For CF and GF nylon, Bambu Lab calls a 0.6 mm hardened nozzle the first choice, 0.4 mm the second, and says not to use 0.2 mm because of clogging. Prusa&#39;s PA11 CF profile uses a 0.4 mm hardened, non-high-flow nozzle.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Printer requirements: hot end, nozzle and enclosure</h2>
<p>Before buying a spool, check four things on your printer:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Nozzle temperature.</strong> Most nylon needs 250–300 °C. Polymaker says nylons that print above 240 °C need an all-metal hot end. Prusa specifically advises against its MINI+ for PA11 CF because of the temperature.</li>
<li><strong>Hardened nozzle for fibre grades.</strong> Bambu Lab marks a hardened steel nozzle as required for every PA-CF, PA6-CF, PAHT-CF and glass-fibre grade and says brass and stainless steel nozzles wear quickly. Plain PA does not need one, according to the same table. See our <a href="/blog/carbon-fiber-filament/">carbon fiber filament guide</a> for more on abrasive filaments.</li>
<li><strong>Bed temperature.</strong> Plan for 80–110 °C for most grades.</li>
<li><strong>Enclosure.</strong> Bambu Lab recommends enclosed printers and says open-frame printers are not recommended for nylon and nylon composites. Prusa says a warm enclosure &quot;helps a lot&quot; against warping, though its PA11 CF prints without one thanks to the fibres. Our <a href="/blog/3d-printer-enclosure/">3D printer enclosure</a> guide covers options for open printers.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Fumes:</strong> Prusa notes that polyamides give off a strong smell and ultrafine particles, and asks for a well-ventilated room or an enclosure, ideally with active filtration. Do not print nylon in a bedroom.</p>
<p><strong>AMS and feeders:</strong> Bambu Lab lists plain PA as AMS-compatible, but among fibre-filled nylons only its own PA-CF and PAHT-CF. Other brands&#39; CF and GF nylons can be too brittle or rough for it.</p>
<h2>Drying nylon filament and keeping it dry</h2>
<p>Moisture is the biggest cause of failed nylon prints. Prusa says badly stored polyamide can absorb water up to 10 % of its weight, and that quality can drop within days of exposure. Polymaker&#39;s PA6-CF data sheet lists an equilibrium water absorption of 3.33 %. Wet nylon pops at the nozzle and prints bubbly, stringy, weak parts.</p>

<h2>Bed surfaces and adhesion for nylon</h2>
<p>Nylon shrinks as it crystallises, so it pulls up at the corners unless it is stuck down well and kept warm.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bambu Lab:</strong> Engineering Plate, smooth PEI (High Temperature) or Textured PEI plate, with glue. It does not recommend the Cool Plate or liquid glue for PA.</li>
<li><strong>Polymaker:</strong> PEI sheets, Magigoo or a glue stick; PC or Textured PEI for PolyMide PA6-CF.</li>
<li><strong>Prusa:</strong> its dedicated PA Nylon sheet, which has a matte, satin-like texture. Prusa says to clean it with water, not isopropyl alcohol or acetone.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> glue on PEI also acts as a release layer, so the part comes off once the plate cools. Add a brim to long or thin parts.</p>
<h2>Troubleshooting nylon prints</h2>
<p><strong>Warping or corners lifting</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Causes: draughts, cold room, bed too cool, no glue, sharp corners on large flat parts.</li>
<li>Fixes, in order: close the enclosure and let the chamber warm up; raise the bed to the top of the maker&#39;s range; apply fresh glue; add a brim; round the corners in your CAD model; switch to a CF/GF or PA12 grade.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Popping, bubbles, stringing or a fuzzy surface</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Cause: wet filament (by far the most common reason).</li>
<li>Fixes: dry the spool; print from a dry box; only then tune temperature or retraction.</li>
<li>Prevention: store sealed with desiccant.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Weak layers that split</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Causes: nozzle too cold, too much part cooling, cold air on the print, wet filament.</li>
<li>Fixes: raise nozzle temperature by 5–10 °C; cut the part fan; enclose the printer; dry the spool.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Clogging or under-extrusion with CF nylon</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Causes: 0.2 mm or worn nozzle, brass nozzle eroded by fibres, heat creep.</li>
<li>Fixes: fit a 0.4–0.6 mm hardened nozzle; check the nozzle for wear; follow the maker&#39;s speed limit.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> pure, unfilled nylon is also useful for cleaning. Prusa notes it is good for &quot;cold pull&quot; nozzle cleaning, because it grips debris well as it cools.</p>
<h2>Uses: what to print with nylon filament</h2>
<p>The Prusa Knowledge Base credits nylon with high mechanical and abrasion resistance, a low friction coefficient and a high melting point, and Polymaker gives continuous use up to about 120 °C for unfilled grades. If your PLA or PETG parts crack, wear at a sliding surface or creep under load, nylon is the usual next step; for sun and weather alone, ASA is far easier.</p>

<h2>Checklist: your first nylon print</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check your printer:</strong> nozzle up to at least 260–290 °C on an all-metal hot end, bed to about 100 °C, and an enclosure or a warm, draught-free room.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the grade:</strong> a CF nylon or PA12 for the first attempt; plain PA6 once you have drying under control.</li>
<li><strong>Fit the right nozzle:</strong> hardened steel, 0.4 or 0.6 mm, for any CF or GF grade.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the spool</strong> at the maker&#39;s setting (for example 80 °C for 8–12 hours for Bambu Lab nylons).</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the bed:</strong> the maker&#39;s recommended plate with a thin layer of glue, or a dedicated nylon sheet.</li>
<li><strong>Load the maker&#39;s profile</strong> or use the middle of the published ranges, part fan low or off.</li>
<li><strong>Let the enclosure warm up</strong> with the bed on, then print from a dry box in a ventilated room.</li>
<li><strong>Run a temperature tower</strong> before any long print, and store the spool sealed afterwards.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>Nylon filament is the right choice when a part must be tough, slippery and hard-wearing – or, in carbon fibre form, stiff and heat resistant. PA6 is the strongest but the thirstiest and the most prone to warping; PA12, PA11 and PAHT-CF absorb less water and print more predictably; CF and GF grades warp least but need a hardened nozzle. Success comes down to an all-metal hot end, a warm enclosure with a glued bed, and filament that is dried and kept dry. If your printer cannot meet those needs, PETG or ASA will serve most parts with far less effort.</p>
