2a:T7ac,<p><strong>PLA as a plastic can be food-safe, but a 3D-printed PLA part usually is not.</strong> The problem is not the material but the way FDM printing builds a part: in layers, with tiny grooves between them where food collects and bacteria grow. Washing does not reach into those grooves reliably.</p>
<p>This guide follows Prusa Research’s published guidance. If you make items for sale or for others, check your local food-contact rules.</p>

<h2>Why layer lines are the real problem</h2>
<p>Even with a perfect, certified filament, the surface of a print has gaps between layers. Prusa’s test of untreated, smoothed and epoxy-coated PLA cups showed why untreated prints are risky. Soap and detergent can also soak into the layer lines and be released later.</p>
<h2>Safer ways to use prints in the kitchen</h2>
<ul>
<li>Print <strong>tools that do not touch food</strong>: holders, organisers, clips and stands.</li>
<li>Use prints as <strong>moulds or cutters once</strong>, then throw them away.</li>
<li><strong>Coat</strong> the food-contact surface with a certified food-safe coating and follow the coating’s curing instructions.</li>
<li>For containers, cups and bottles, <strong>buy food-grade products</strong> instead. Prusa does not recommend 3D prints as food containers.</li>
</ul>
