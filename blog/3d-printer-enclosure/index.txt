28:T3209,<p><strong>A 3D printer enclosure is a box, tent or cabinet around the printer that keeps warm, still air around the print – you need one for warp-prone materials like ABS, ASA, PC and nylon, but not for PLA.</strong> An enclosure also cuts draughts, dust and some noise, and it keeps hands and pets away from hot, moving parts. It brings its own problems too: heat that electronics and PLA dislike, fumes that collect inside, and fire-safety questions if you build it yourself. This guide explains when an enclosure helps, the main types (tents, DIY table builds, cabinets and printers with a built-in chamber), heat limits, ventilation and filtration, and fire safety. Material advice and printer features come from the makers&#39; own documentation (Bambu Lab Wiki, Prusa Knowledge Base and blog, Creality and Prusa product pages), checked November 2026. We do not review or rank individual enclosure products.</p>

<h2>Why use a 3D printer enclosure?</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Warping and layer splitting.</strong> ABS, ASA, PC and nylon shrink as they cool. If the air around the part is cold or moving, the bottom corners lift and tall parts crack between layers. Bambu Lab says these materials need a higher bed and chamber temperature to suppress warping. Prusa&#39;s enclosure guide notes that even about 35 °C inside an enclosure makes a large difference for ABS.</li>
<li><strong>Draughts.</strong> Air conditioning, open windows and fans cool one side of a print faster than the other. An enclosure blocks them, which helps even with PETG in a cold garage.</li>
<li><strong>Dust and safety.</strong> A closed box keeps dust off rods and belts and keeps children and pets away from the hot nozzle and bed.</li>
<li><strong>Noise.</strong> Panels damp some fan and motor noise. They do not make a printer silent, especially if you add an exhaust fan.</li>
<li><strong>Fumes.</strong> An enclosure contains fumes and particles in one place, where a fan or filter can deal with them. On its own it does not clean the air.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you print only PLA and PETG in a warm, still room, you probably do not need an enclosure for print quality. Buy or build one when you start with ABS or ASA – see our <a href="/blog/asa-filament/">ASA filament guide</a>.</p>
<h2>Does PLA need an enclosure?</h2>
<p>No, and a closed, warm enclosure can make PLA worse. PLA has the lowest glass transition temperature of the common filaments, and Bambu Lab&#39;s wiki calls it the filament most likely to clog through heat creep – the hotend&#39;s cold zone gets too warm, the filament softens early and jams. Bambu Lab advises keeping the temperature inside an enclosure at least 10 degrees below the filament&#39;s glass transition temperature, and says printing PLA with the front door open and the top cover removed gives better results on its enclosed printers. Prusa&#39;s enclosure article gives the same advice: leave the enclosure open for PLA so the part-cooling fan gets cool air.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if your printer lives in an enclosure all the time, open the door or lift the lid for PLA jobs, or fit a vent fan that you can switch on.</p>

<p>The two-table build is a long-running DIY favourite. Prusa describes one made from two or three flat-pack side tables and 3 mm acrylic sheets, and also mentions a textile photo-studio tent as a no-build alternative. In its tent test the inside reached around 38 °C after ten minutes of printing at a room temperature of 26 °C – with heat from the printer alone.</p>
<p>Commercial kits go further. The Original Prusa Enclosure uses a metal frame with metal top and bottom panels and PETG side panels, and offers an optional HEPA filter and an optional automatic fire-suppression system that works without power by sensing high temperature. Prusa notes the enclosure is passive: the heat comes only from the printer&#39;s heatbed.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> pick the type by what you print most. For occasional ABS on an open-frame printer, a simple build is fine. For regular engineering materials, a printer with a built-in, filtered chamber saves a lot of DIY work.</p>
<h2>Built-in enclosures and heated chambers</h2>
<p>Many current CoreXY printers come enclosed. Some only trap the printer&#39;s own heat; others heat the chamber actively and filter the air:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prusa Core One:</strong> actively controlled chamber up to 55 °C. Prusa lists ABS, ASA, HIPS and PA as needing its optional Advanced Filtration System. See our <a href="/printers/prusa-core-one/">Prusa Core One page</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Creality K2 Plus:</strong> active chamber heating up to 60 °C and two air filters.</li>
<li><strong>Bambu Lab X1 Carbon:</strong> enclosed, with an activated carbon filter.</li>
</ul>
<p>A passive enclosure&#39;s temperature depends on the room and the print length; an actively heated chamber holds a set temperature, which matters most for large ABS, ASA, PC and nylon parts. Check each printer&#39;s spec sheet for an enclosure, chamber heating and filtration before you buy.</p>
<h2>Heat limits: electronics, power supplies and PLA</h2>
<p>Heat is the main design problem of any 3D printer enclosure.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Power supply:</strong> Prusa&#39;s enclosure guide says the power supply must stay outside, because the walls raise the inside temperature above its recommended operating temperature. Prusa&#39;s DIY article adds that keeping a power supply above 40–50 °C can significantly shorten its life.</li>
<li><strong>Mainboard, screen and motors:</strong> printers built open-frame were not designed for a warm box. Mount the screen outside if you can, and keep the mainboard&#39;s cooling fan breathing cool air.</li>
<li><strong>PLA prints and parts:</strong> Bambu Lab&#39;s material guide says PLA is not recommended for parts used around 50 °C or higher. Avoid PLA brackets, hinges or fan ducts inside a chamber that runs hot.</li>
<li><strong>Filament:</strong> do not store spare spools inside a warm enclosure. Keep them sealed elsewhere – see our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-store-filament/">how to store filament</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> put a cheap thermometer inside the enclosure and check it during a long ABS print. If it climbs far above what you need, vent some heat rather than cooking the electronics.</p>
<h2>Ventilation and filtration</h2>
<p>An enclosure does not remove fumes; it gives you one place to deal with them. ABS and ASA give off more fumes and smell than PLA. UL Chemical Insights advises printing in a room with good airflow or windows that open, keeping the printer away from return-air vents and shared living spaces, and staying away from it while it runs.</p>
<p>Your options:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Exhaust to outside.</strong> A small fan pulls air out of the enclosure through a duct to a window. Slow the fan or vent only after the print for ABS, because strong airflow cools the chamber and brings warping back. Prusa&#39;s DIY guide suggests opening a window to vent the fumes after the print finishes.</li>
<li><strong>Filtration inside.</strong> Activated carbon targets smells and gases; HEPA targets fine particles. Some enclosures and printers offer both, others only one. Filters wear out, so follow the maker&#39;s replacement schedule.</li>
<li><strong>Room ventilation.</strong> Even with a filter, print in a room you can air out, and not where you sleep.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for ABS, ASA and nylon, a filtered or vented enclosed printer is the safer long-term setup than an open printer in a living room.</p>
<h2>Fire safety</h2>
<p>Printers rarely catch fire, but a DIY enclosure puts heat sources inside a box made of materials that can burn.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mount a smoke detector</strong> inside or directly above the enclosure. Prusa&#39;s DIY build includes a fire and smoke detector at the back of the top table.</li>
<li><strong>Keep electronics and the power supply outside</strong> where possible, and use the printer&#39;s original wiring and connectors.</li>
<li><strong>Prefer non-combustible or flame-resistant materials</strong> for frames and panels – metal frames, fire-rated fabrics – especially around the hotend.</li>
<li><strong>Keep flammable items away:</strong> paper, curtains and spare spools do not belong next to the printer.</li>
<li><strong>Consider fire suppression</strong> for printers that run unattended. Self-contained suppression units are sold for enclosures such as Prusa&#39;s.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the firmware&#39;s thermal protection enabled</strong> and do not leave a printer running unattended until you trust it.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How to set up a 3D printer enclosure – checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Decide if you need one:</strong> ABS, ASA, PC or nylon – yes; PLA and TPU – no; PETG – only if the room is cold or draughty.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the type:</strong> tent or DIY table build for occasional use, kit or enclosed printer for regular engineering materials.</li>
<li><strong>Measure:</strong> printer footprint plus bed travel, spool holder, cable routes and room to open doors.</li>
<li><strong>Move the power supply and, if possible, the screen outside.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Plan airflow:</strong> an exhaust duct to a window or a carbon and HEPA filter, and a way to open the enclosure for PLA.</li>
<li><strong>Add safety:</strong> smoke detector, clear space around the enclosure, no flammable items nearby.</li>
<li><strong>Put it on a solid base</strong> – our <a href="/blog/3d-printer-stand/">3D printer stand guide</a> covers load and stability.</li>
<li><strong>Test with a thermometer</strong> during a long print and adjust the vent until the chamber is warm enough for ABS but not too hot for the electronics.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>You need a 3D printer enclosure when you print ABS, ASA, PC or nylon; for PLA it is optional and should be left open or vented. A simple tent or two-table build works for occasional ABS on an open-frame printer, provided the power supply stays outside, a smoke detector is fitted and fumes are vented. If engineering materials are your main use, a printer with a built-in, filtered and preferably heated chamber is the less fiddly choice. For the materials themselves, see our <a href="/blog/asa-vs-abs/">ASA vs ABS comparison</a>.</p>
