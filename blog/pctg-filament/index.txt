This guide explains what PCTG is, how it really differs from PETG, the settings makers publish, how to get clear prints and where it makes sense. We have not tested PCTG ourselves; your spool label and slicer profile come first.</p>

<h2>What is PCTG?</h2>
<p>PCTG and PETG are both modified versions of PET, the polyester used for drink bottles. The difference is in the glycol part of the chain. In standard PET it is ethylene glycol. In PETG some of it is replaced with a bulkier molecule, cyclohexanedimethanol (CHDM), which stops the plastic crystallising and makes it easier to print. As Hackaday explains it, PETG replaces less than half of the glycol with CHDM, while PCTG replaces more than half.</p>
<p>That extra CHDM is what changes the behaviour: PCTG is more ductile, takes impacts better and, according to makers, resists chemicals and heat slightly better. It is still an amorphous copolyester, so it prints with the same habits as PETG – no enclosure, low warping, good layer bonding.</p>
<p><strong>What does PCTG stand for?</strong> Polycyclohexylenedimethylene terephthalate, glycol-modified. Some brands write &quot;PCTG Pro&quot; or &quot;Pro PCTG&quot;; these are brand names, not separate materials.</p>

<p>The impact claims are the makers&#39; own marketing figures, so treat the multipliers with care. An independent comparison summarised by Hackaday in 2026 – tests by the My Tech Fun channel on transparent PETG and PCTG from the same maker – found PCTG more flexible and ductile, much more impact-resistant and better at holding up to heat, while needing higher print temperatures, closer to ASA presets.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if your PETG parts crack at snap-fits, screw bosses or on impact, PCTG is the obvious thing to try first. If they fail by softening in heat, compare with <a href="/blog/asa-filament/">ASA filament</a> too. Our <a href="/blog/petg-filament/">PETG filament guide</a> covers the material PCTG is replacing.</p>

<p>The ranges overlap: a 260 °C nozzle and an 80 °C bed sit inside all three. Spectrum&#39;s sheet also confirms that plain PCTG needs no hardened or ruby nozzle.</p>
<p>A few extra points from 3D-Fuel&#39;s profile notes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Calibrate pressure advance</strong> (linear advance) for PCTG; do not reuse a PETG value.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid grid infill</strong> – 3D-Fuel says it can make PCTG build up on the nozzle; use gyroid or cubic.</li>
<li><strong>Do not share a nozzle with PLA.</strong> PLA residue can coat the PCTG and weaken layer bonding. Purge well or clean the nozzle when switching.</li>
<li><strong>Lower cooling helps layer bonding.</strong> 3D-Fuel reduced fan speeds in its later profiles for this reason.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> start in the middle of the overlap – 260 °C, 80 °C bed on textured PEI, low fan, outer walls at 50 mm/s – then print a temperature tower and adjust in 5 °C steps.</p>
<h2>Bed adhesion: PCTG sticks very hard</h2>
<p>The most common PCTG problem is not poor adhesion but too much. Fiberlogy warns not to print directly on the bare bed or glass because the bond can damage the surface, and recommends masking tape. 3D-Fuel recommends textured PEI and warns against smooth PEI, where parts stick too well.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Textured PEI:</strong> the usual choice; let the plate cool fully before removing parts.</li>
<li><strong>Smooth PEI or glass:</strong> use a release layer – glue stick, PVA glue or tape.</li>
<li><strong>Do not over-squash</strong> the first layer; it makes the bond even stronger.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Clear PCTG prints</h2>
<p>Clarity is one of PCTG&#39;s selling points: Spectrum says its transparent colours are clearer than standard PETG, and Fiberlogy lists high optical transparency. Printed parts will never be glass-clear, because every layer line and infill wall scatters light. Practical ways to get the clearest result:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use a transparent colour and print hollow or with 100 % infill rather than a sparse pattern.</li>
<li>Keep walls few and thick, or print in vase mode for shades and covers.</li>
<li>Print slower and slightly hotter so lines fuse fully, with low cooling.</li>
<li>Dry the spool – moisture bubbles turn clear prints cloudy.</li>
</ul>
<p>These are general techniques for clear copolyesters; makers do not publish clarity figures for printed parts.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> no maker sells PCTG as a stiffer plastic – the flexural modulus on these sheets is 1600–1722 MPa, well below fibre-filled grades. What it adds is toughness: parts bend and absorb hits instead of cracking.</p>
<h2>Drying and storing PCTG</h2>
<p>Copolyesters absorb moisture from the air, and wet PCTG strings, bubbles and loses clarity. Fiberlogy gives 60 °C for 4 hours; 3D-Fuel gives 70 °C for 6–8 hours when needed, followed by sealed storage with desiccant. Both temperatures are within the range of most filament dryers. Our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-dry-filament/">how to dry filament</a> covers dryers, ovens and storage.</p>
<h2>What PCTG is used for</h2>
<p>Makers and independent tests point to the same jobs:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Impact parts:</strong> clips, snap-fits, protective covers, tool holders, RC and drone parts.</li>
<li><strong>Enclosures and cases:</strong> electronics housings, chemical-resistant boxes (3D-Fuel lists resistance to alcohols, fuels, oils, bleach and degreasers).</li>
<li><strong>Jigs and fixtures:</strong> shop tooling that gets dropped and clamped.</li>
<li><strong>Automotive parts:</strong> brackets and interior trim, within its heat limit.</li>
<li><strong>Clear parts:</strong> lamp shades, light covers, bottle cages, display boxes.</li>
<li><strong>Springs and compliant mechanisms:</strong> Hackaday&#39;s summary suggests PCTG&#39;s ductility suits them.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Food contact:</strong> Fiberlogy says its PCTG material is suitable for food contact. A printed part is still porous at the layer lines; see our guide on <a href="/blog/is-pla-food-safe/">whether PLA is food safe</a> – the same limits apply to PCTG prints.</p>
<h2>How to print PCTG – checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Read the label</strong> and the maker&#39;s data sheet; ranges vary by brand.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the spool</strong> at 60–70 °C if it has been open.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare the bed:</strong> textured PEI, or a release layer on smooth PEI or glass.</li>
<li><strong>Load a PCTG profile</strong> from the maker, or start from PETG and raise the nozzle to about 260 °C.</li>
<li><strong>Lower part cooling</strong> below your PETG value.</li>
<li><strong>Slow outer walls</strong> to about 50 mm/s for a clean surface.</li>
<li><strong>Calibrate pressure advance and flow</strong> for PCTG specifically.</li>
<li><strong>Switch grid infill</strong> to gyroid or cubic.</li>
<li><strong>Print a temperature tower</strong> and a small functional test part.</li>
<li><strong>Let the bed cool</strong> before removing the print.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>PCTG filament is PETG with more toughness: the same easy, enclosure-free printing at about 10–20 °C more nozzle heat, with parts that bend instead of crack and clear colours that look a little cleaner. It is not stiffer and not dramatically more heat-resistant than PETG, and its very strong bond to smooth build plates needs a textured plate or a release layer. For snap-fits, impact parts, cases and clear covers it is a sensible upgrade; for stiffness choose a fibre-filled grade, and for outdoor heat compare ASA.</p>
