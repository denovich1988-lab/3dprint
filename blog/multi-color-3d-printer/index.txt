This guide explains the three designs, compares the current multicolour systems from Bambu Lab, Creality, Prusa, Anycubic, Snapmaker, Elegoo, Flashforge and QIDI using the makers&#39; own specs, and ends with a step-by-step way to choose. We do not test printers and prices change, so we compare features, not prices. Facts checked November 2026.</p>

<h2>How a filament changer works</h2>
<p>A filament changer holds four spools (more with chained units) and pushes the selected filament to a single nozzle. For each colour change, the printer cuts the filament, pulls the old colour back to the changer, loads the new one and purges the remaining old colour out of the nozzle – into a waste chute or onto a prime tower – until the new colour runs clean. Bambu Lab&#39;s AMS is the best-known example; our explainer on <a href="/blog/what-is-ams-in-3d-printing/">what AMS is in 3D printing</a> shows the feeders, hub and buffer in detail.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> this is the cheapest way to get four or more colours, and many printers are sold as combos with one included. The trade-off is purge waste and time: a print with hundreds of colour changes can waste a noticeable amount of filament and take much longer than the single-colour version.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> in Bambu Studio, OrcaSlicer and similar slicers, you can flush into infill or into a support object instead of purging everything into the waste chute. Bambu Lab&#39;s wiki on reducing waste during filament change explains the options.</p>
<h2>Dual nozzle and IDEX printers</h2>
<p>A dual-nozzle printer has two hot ends with their own filament paths. Bambu Lab&#39;s H2D uses two nozzles on one toolhead and can still add AMS units for more colours. IDEX (independent dual extruder) printers such as the Flashforge Creator Pro 2 move two heads separately, so the idle head parks out of the way. The UltiMaker S5 uses two swappable print cores.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> two colours or two materials – for example a body in PLA and soluble or breakaway supports in a support material – print with little waste. For more than two colours, dual-nozzle machines rely on filament changers as well.</p>
<h2>Toolchanger printers</h2>
<p>A toolchanger stores several complete print heads and picks the one it needs. Each colour or material stays in its own nozzle, so there is no purge of old colour, and each head can be set to a different temperature.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prusa XL</strong> – a CoreXY toolchanger with 1, 2 or 5 toolheads and a 360 × 360 × 360 mm build volume. See our <a href="/printers/prusa-xl/">Prusa XL page</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Prusa INDX</strong> – a toolchanger for the CORE One family with 4 or 8 passive, induction-heated tools. Prusa says a change takes about 12 seconds and primes only about 13 mg of filament. See <a href="/printers/prusa-indx/">Prusa INDX</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Snapmaker U1</strong> – four independent toolheads on a 270 × 270 × 270 mm CoreXY printer.</li>
<li><strong>Creality K3</strong> – four docked nozzles (Creality&#39;s KliTek system), each with its own filament path; Creality lists a nozzle swap in about 5 seconds.</li>
<li><strong>Bambu Lab H2C</strong> – the Vortek system stores six induction-heated hot ends that swap onto the toolhead, and works with AMS 2 Pro and AMS HT units for up to 24 filaments.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> if you print multicolour models often, the time and filament saved per change adds up. Toolchangers are also better for combining very different materials, such as PLA with TPU or PETG with a support material.</p>

<h2>Multicolour vs multi-material</h2>
<p>A multicolour print uses the same material in different colours – four colours of PLA, for example. Multi-material printing mixes different plastics, such as PLA with soluble support, or a rigid body with flexible TPU grips. Single-nozzle changers handle multicolour PLA well but have limits on materials: Bambu Lab, for example, does not recommend soft TPU, damp PVA or many fibre-filled filaments in the standard AMS. Toolchangers and dual-nozzle printers are more flexible because each material has its own nozzle and temperature.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you mainly want soluble or easy-release supports, a dual-nozzle printer or a toolchanger is often the better buy than a large filament changer.</p>
<h2>What multicolour printing costs you in time and filament</h2>
<p>On a single-nozzle system, each layer that contains two colours needs at least one change. A model with colours on every layer – a figurine with a striped pattern, for example – may need hundreds or thousands of changes. Every change adds the cut, unload, load and purge sequence, and every purge throws away some filament.</p>
<p>Ways to reduce it:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Group colours by height.</strong> Models whose colours change by layer (a sign with coloured text on top) need far fewer changes than models with colours mixed on every layer.</li>
<li><strong>Flush into infill or a support object</strong> in the slicer.</li>
<li><strong>Print several copies at once</strong> – the purge is shared across all copies on the plate.</li>
<li><strong>Choose a toolchanger</strong> if you print complex multicolour models often.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Files and slicing for multicolour prints</h2>
<p>Multicolour models are usually shared as 3MF files, which store the colour assignment for each part; an STL holds only geometry, so colours must be painted in the slicer. Our <a href="/3mf-viewer/">3MF viewer</a> shows what a 3MF project contains before you open it in a slicer, and <a href="/3mf-to-stl/">3MF to STL</a> exports parts when you need plain meshes.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you have no multicolour hardware, you can still print in layers of colour by adding a filament change at a set height in the slicer; the printer pauses and you swap the spool by hand.</p>
<h2>How to choose a multi color 3D printer</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Decide how many colours you really need.</strong> Four is enough for most models; more only helps if you print complex art pieces or keep several materials loaded.</li>
<li><strong>Decide how often you will print in colour.</strong> Occasional multicolour prints suit a filament changer; daily multicolour work justifies a toolchanger.</li>
<li><strong>Check materials.</strong> If you want TPU, soluble supports or engineering plastics alongside PLA, prefer a dual nozzle or toolchanger, and read the changer&#39;s supported materials list.</li>
<li><strong>Check build size and enclosure.</strong> Multicolour does not change the basics: a larger bed and an enclosure matter just as much as on a single-colour printer.</li>
<li><strong>Check expandability.</strong> Some printers accept extra changer units later; others are fixed at four colours.</li>
<li><strong>Check the slicer.</strong> Make sure the maker&#39;s slicer, or OrcaSlicer, supports painting colours and reducing purge.</li>
<li><strong>Compare specs side by side</strong> with our <a href="/printers/compare/">printer comparison tool</a>.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>For most people, a printer sold with a four-spool filament changer is the easiest and most affordable way into multi color 3D printing. If you print multicolour or multi-material parts every day, or want to mix very different plastics, a toolchanger such as the Prusa XL, Prusa INDX, Snapmaker U1, Creality K3 or Bambu Lab H2C saves filament and time on every change.</p>
