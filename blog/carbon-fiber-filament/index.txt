<h2>What is carbon fiber filament?</h2>
<p>A carbon fiber filament – often written CF filament or carbon fiber reinforced filament – is a base thermoplastic compounded with short carbon fibres. Bambu Lab gives the fibre length in its filaments as roughly 0.05–0.2 mm, and Polymaker lists its PLA-CF as 8 % chopped carbon fibre by weight. The fibres do not melt at printing temperatures; they travel through the nozzle as solid particles inside the molten plastic.</p>
<p>Prusa&#39;s knowledge base sums up the effect: fibres generally improve dimensional stability, reduce weight and warping, and raise heat resistance and tensile yield strength, but they usually reduce impact resistance and layer-to-layer adhesion.</p>
<p><strong>Watch out:</strong> not every &quot;carbon&quot; spool is reinforced. Prusa says the fibres in its own PETG Carbon Fiber are there for looks only. Read the data sheet rather than the colour name.</p>

<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you have never printed carbon fiber, start with PLA-CF or PETG-CF. They print on an open printer at familiar temperatures. Our <a href="/blog/pla-filament/">PLA filament guide</a> and <a href="/blog/petg-filament/">PETG filament guide</a> cover the base plastics.</p>
<h2>Why carbon fiber filament needs a hardened nozzle</h2>
<p>Carbon fibres are hard and abrasive. Pushed through a soft brass nozzle, they wear the bore wider, which ruins extrusion and dimensional accuracy. That is why:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Bambu Lab&#39;s material table</strong> lists a hardened steel nozzle as required for every carbon- and glass-fibre material, including PLA-CF, and warns that brass and stainless steel nozzles wear quickly with abrasive fillers.</li>
<li><strong>Prusa</strong> lists &quot;requires a hardened steel nozzle&quot; as the first con of composite filaments.</li>
<li><strong>Polymaker</strong> recommends hardened steel, ruby or diamond-coated nozzles and notes that abrasive fibres also speed up wear on extruder gears and Bowden tubes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Some printers now ship with a hardened steel nozzle – Bambu Lab cites the <a href="/printers/bambu-lab-x1-carbon/">X1 Carbon</a> as an example – while many budget printers ship with brass. Check your printer&#39;s spec page before loading a CF spool.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> steel conducts heat less well than brass. If extrusion feels weak after a nozzle swap, raise the temperature a little and slow down, and recalibrate flow.</p>
<h2>Nozzle size and clogging</h2>
<p>Fibres do not melt, so a narrow nozzle can jam. Bambu Lab says the chance of clogging a 0.2 mm nozzle with these materials is very high and advises against it. Its first choice for most fibre grades is a 0.6 mm nozzle, with 0.4 mm second; for its own PLA-CF and PETG-CF, 0.4 mm is the first choice. Prusa gives 0.4 mm and 0.2 mm layers as the lowest optimal values, and Simplify3D notes that nozzles of 0.5 mm or more clog less often.</p>
<p>Other ways to prevent jams, from the same sources:</p>
<ul>
<li>Clean the nozzle before switching to CF. Prusa recommends a cold pull; Bambu Lab heats the nozzle to 280 °C and clears it with the supplied tools.</li>
<li>Do not squash the first layer too hard. Prusa and Simplify3D both warn that a nozzle set too close builds back-pressure and clogs.</li>
<li>Keep the filament path smooth, without sharp bends or rubbing edges.</li>
<li>Use thicker layers rather than very fine ones.</li>
</ul>
<p>If a jam happens anyway, our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-clean-a-3d-printer-nozzle/">how to clean a 3D printer nozzle</a> walks through cold pulls and needle cleaning.</p>

<p>What this means in practice:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stiffness goes up.</strong> PLA-CF bends less than PETG-CF and, per Bambu Lab, more than 30 % less than plain PLA.</li>
<li><strong>The Z axis is the weak spot.</strong> Across the layers, both materials are far weaker than along them. Orient parts so the main load runs along the layers.</li>
<li><strong>Toughness goes down.</strong> Prusa notes lower impact resistance; Bambu Lab calls PET-CF brittle. A stiff CF bracket can snap where a plain PETG one would bend.</li>
<li><strong>Heat resistance follows the base plastic.</strong> PLA-CF still softens in the mid-50s °C. For a car interior or near a motor, choose PETG-CF, nylon-CF or higher.</li>
<li><strong>Nylon changes with moisture.</strong> Bambu Lab says PA6 absorbs about 3 % water and PA12 about 0.3 %; wet nylon loses strength and stiffness but gains toughness.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> compare data sheets only when they use the same test method and orientation. A headline &quot;up to&quot; number from one maker is not comparable with another&#39;s average.</p>
<h2>Drying and storing carbon fiber filament</h2>
<p>The fibres do not absorb water, but the base plastic does. Bambu Lab recommends drying PLA-CF at 55 °C for 8 hours and nylon-CF at 80 °C for 8–12 hours, and keeping its CF spools below 20 % relative humidity with desiccant. Polymaker lists nylon-CF and PC-CF at 70–80 °C for 4–6 hours. Nylon-based grades are the most sensitive: wet nylon strings, oozes and can clog the nozzle. See <a href="/blog/how-to-dry-filament/">how to dry filament</a> for methods.</p>
<p>Use filament feeders with care. Bambu Lab lists its own PLA-CF, PA-CF and PAHT-CF as AMS-compatible, but PET-CF as not, and Polymaker advises against running its PLA-CF through an AMS. CF filament is also more brittle, so avoid tight bends in the path.</p>
<h2>Safety: dust, fumes and sharp ends</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Ventilation.</strong> NIOSH notes concerns about ultrafine particles and chemicals from 3D printing, and UL Chemical Insights advises printing in a room with good airflow, away from where you sleep. Fibre grades on ABS, ASA, nylon or PC bases need the same care as their base plastic, or more.</li>
<li><strong>Sanding and cutting.</strong> SUNLU advises avoiding the dust from sanding its PLA-CF. If you sand, file or drill CF parts, wet-sand or use a dust mask and clean up with a vacuum rather than a brush.</li>
<li><strong>Food contact.</strong> Treat CF filaments as not food-safe unless the maker&#39;s data says otherwise; Polymaker states no food-safety data is available for its PLA-CF.</li>
<li><strong>Sharp fragments.</strong> Broken filament ends and snapped CF parts can be stiff and sharp. Wear gloves when clearing jams in the extruder.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How to print carbon fiber filament – checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check the nozzle.</strong> Fit a hardened steel (or ruby, tungsten carbide) nozzle; 0.4 mm for PLA-CF and PETG-CF, 0.6 mm for engineering grades. Never 0.2 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Clean the hot end</strong> with a cold pull or the maker&#39;s cleaning routine.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the spool</strong> at the maker&#39;s temperature – 55 °C for PLA-CF, 65–70 °C for PETG-CF, 80 °C for nylon-CF.</li>
<li><strong>Pick the profile.</strong> Use the maker&#39;s CF profile, or the base plastic&#39;s profile with the CF temperature range from the label.</li>
<li><strong>Slow down</strong> 25–50 % compared with the plain plastic, or follow the maker&#39;s volumetric limit.</li>
<li><strong>Set a normal first layer</strong> – not squashed – and a layer height of 0.2 mm or more.</li>
<li><strong>Orient for strength.</strong> Keep the main load along the layers, not across them.</li>
<li><strong>Enclose</strong> nylon, PC, ABS and ASA grades; PLA-CF and PETG-CF print on open printers.</li>
<li><strong>Inspect the nozzle</strong> now and then. If prints start under-extruding or getting wider, the nozzle may be worn.</li>
</ol>
<p>Before you print a functional part, check its wall thickness and size in an STL viewer, and remember that thin walls in a CF part are stiff but can snap.</p>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>Carbon fiber filament is the right choice when you need a stiff, accurate, light part with a clean matte finish – drone arms, RC parts, jigs and brackets. It is not a magic strength upgrade: it trades impact resistance and layer adhesion for stiffness, and its heat resistance stays close to the base plastic. Fit a hardened nozzle, keep it to 0.4 mm or larger, dry the spool and choose the base plastic for the job – PLA-CF for easy stiff parts, PETG-CF for tougher ones, nylon-CF and up for heat and real loads.</p>
