<h2>How a lithophane works</h2>
<p>A lithophane is a height map of a photo. Each pixel&#39;s brightness is turned into a thickness: dark pixels become thick plastic, bright pixels become thin plastic. Unlit, the panel looks like a white relief with no obvious picture. When light shines through from behind, the thin areas glow and the thick ones stay dark, and the photo appears.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> because the effect depends on light passing through the plastic, filament colour, infill and thickness range matter more than they do for ordinary prints.</p>
<h2>Step 1: choose the right photo</h2>
<p>The best lithophanes start with the right image:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>High contrast</strong> – a clear difference between light and dark areas.</li>
<li><strong>A clear subject</strong> – one or two faces, a pet, a building or a landscape with a strong outline.</li>
<li><strong>Good lighting</strong> – evenly lit faces without deep shadows that turn into solid blocks.</li>
<li><strong>Not too busy</strong> – fine textures such as grass or crowds turn into noise at small sizes.</li>
<li><strong>Sharp focus</strong> – blur is magnified when backlit.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> crop tightly to the subject and bump contrast slightly in a photo editor before converting. Converting to greyscale first helps you see how the tones will look.</p>
<h2>Step 2: turn the photo into a lithophane STL</h2>
<p>Open our <a href="/lithophane-maker/">lithophane maker</a>, choose your JPG or PNG and adjust:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Width</strong> – the panel&#39;s width in millimetres. Our tool starts at 100 mm, a good size for a first print.</li>
<li><strong>Base (minimum thickness)</strong> – the thinnest part of the panel, for the brightest pixels. The default is 0.8 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Extra thickness</strong> – how much is added for the darkest pixels. The default adds 2.4 mm, for a maximum of 3.2 mm.</li>
<li><strong>Dark = thick</strong> – keep this on for a normal lithophane; turn it off for a negative effect.</li>
<li><strong>Detail (resolution)</strong> – higher values keep more detail but make larger files.</li>
</ol>
<p>The preview shows the relief and a backlit simulation as you change settings, and the STL downloads straight from your browser – the photo is never uploaded. For raised reliefs and logos instead of lithophanes, use the <a href="/image-to-stl/">image to STL converter</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> the thickness range controls contrast. A wider range gives deeper shadows; a thinner base gives brighter highlights but a more fragile panel.</p>
<h2>Step 3: slicer settings for a lithophane</h2>
<p>Import the STL into your slicer and set:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Infill: 100%.</strong> With lower infill, the pattern shows through as lines and shadows.</li>
<li><strong>Layer height: 0.12–0.16 mm.</strong> Fine layers improve tonal steps. Our tool&#39;s FAQ gives the same range.</li>
<li><strong>Wall count:</strong> enough walls that the thinnest areas print as solid perimeters; many slicers handle this automatically at 100% infill.</li>
<li><strong>Speed:</strong> moderate; slow the outer walls for a clean surface.</li>
<li><strong>Cooling:</strong> normal PLA cooling.</li>
<li><strong>Seam:</strong> set the seam to the back or an edge so it does not cross the picture.</li>
<li><strong>Brim:</strong> add a brim, or use a lithophane with a thicker frame and base, so the tall panel does not wobble.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> run a small test – a 50 mm wide version of the same photo – before printing a large lithophane. It shows within half an hour whether the contrast and thickness range work.</p>
<h2>Step 4: orientation – vertical vs flat</h2>
<p>Most guides, including printer makers&#39; tutorials, recommend printing lithophanes <strong>standing upright</strong>. In this orientation, the thickness changes are made by the nozzle moving in and out within each layer, which gives smooth gradients. Printed flat, the thickness is built from stacked layers, so tones step in 0.12–0.2 mm jumps and can look banded.</p>
<p>Flat printing is faster and more stable, and some people prefer it for small or very wide lithophanes. If you print flat, use the finest layer height your printer handles well.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> a tall thin panel can wobble as the nozzle moves. A frame around the picture, a wider foot or a brim keeps it stable.</p>
<h2>Best filament for a 3D printed lithophane</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>White PLA</strong> – the standard. It diffuses light evenly and gives the widest range of tones. See our <a href="/blog/pla-filament/">PLA filament guide</a> for settings.</li>
<li><strong>Natural or translucent PLA</strong> – glows brightly but loses contrast; the picture can look washed out.</li>
<li><strong>Silk PLA</strong> – the sheen can create hot spots; test first.</li>
<li><strong>Coloured filaments</strong> – tint the picture; light colours work, dark colours block too much light.</li>
<li><strong>PETG</strong> – works, but it is more translucent and strings more.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> different white filaments transmit light differently. Once you find a white that works, note it and stick with it for consistent results.</p>
<h2>Step 5: lighting and display</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use LED light.</strong> LEDs are bright and stay cool. Avoid hot incandescent or halogen bulbs close to PLA, which softens at relatively low temperatures.</li>
<li><strong>Warm or neutral white</strong> suits portraits; cool white suits landscapes and architecture.</li>
<li><strong>Diffuse the light.</strong> A small gap between the light and the panel, or a diffuser sheet, avoids a bright spot in the middle.</li>
<li><strong>Display options:</strong> window frames (daylight works well), light boxes, desk stands with an LED strip, night lights, and curved or cylindrical lamp shades.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Shapes and project ideas</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flat panels</strong> in a frame or stand – the simplest and most common.</li>
<li><strong>Curved panels</strong> that stand on their own and spread light evenly.</li>
<li><strong>Cylinder lamps</strong> with a photo all the way round.</li>
<li><strong>Heart, round and keepsake shapes</strong> for gifts.</li>
<li><strong>Moon lamps</strong> – spheres printed with a moon surface height map work on the same principle.</li>
<li><strong>Colour lithophanes</strong> – advanced projects that layer coloured filaments behind a white lithophane; they need several filaments and specialised tools.</li>
</ul>
<p>Our lithophane maker makes flat panels; for curved and cylindrical shapes, use a slicer or CAD tool that can bend a panel, or download ready-made models. For more project ideas, see <a href="/blog/what-to-3d-print/">what to 3D print</a>.</p>

<ol>
<li>Pick a bright, high-contrast photo with a clear subject and crop it.</li>
<li>Make the STL in the <a href="/lithophane-maker/">lithophane maker</a> at about 100 mm wide with the default thickness.</li>
<li>Slice with white PLA, 100% infill and 0.12–0.16 mm layers.</li>

<li>Print a small test first, then the full-size panel.</li>
<li>Light it from behind with an LED and adjust the thickness range if needed.</li>
</ol>

<p>A good lithophane 3D print comes down to three things: a bright, high-contrast photo, a sensible thickness range (about 0.8 mm to 3 mm) and the right print settings – white PLA, 100% infill, fine layers and an upright panel.
