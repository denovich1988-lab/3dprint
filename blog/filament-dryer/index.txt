2a:T145e,<p><strong>A filament dryer is a small heated box that pulls moisture out of 3D printer filament, so it prints cleanly again.</strong> Most plastics used for printing absorb water from the air. When wet filament reaches a hot nozzle, the water turns to steam, and you get popping, stringing, rough walls and weak layers.</p>
<p>This guide explains which filaments need drying, how to use a dryer safely and how to keep spools dry afterwards. Drying temperatures differ by brand, so always check the label on your spool.</p>
<h2>Signs your filament is wet</h2>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Symptom</th>
<th>What you see or hear</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody><tr>
<td>Popping or hissing</td>
<td>Small cracks and sizzling sounds at the nozzle while printing</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Steam</td>
<td>Thin wisps rising from the nozzle</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Stringing</td>
<td>Far more hairs between parts than the same settings gave before</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Rough surface</td>
<td>Bubbles, pits or a fuzzy finish on walls</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Brittle filament</td>
<td>PLA that snaps when you bend it near the spool</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Weak parts</td>
<td>Layers that split apart with little force</td>
</tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>If two or more of these appear on a spool that used to print well, drying is the first thing to try before changing slicer settings.</p>
<h2>Which filaments need a dryer</h2>
<p>Water absorption varies a lot between materials:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>TPU and other flexibles</strong> – absorb moisture quickly. Prusa notes that flexible filaments tend to absorb moisture and are hard to print when wet.</li>
<li><strong>Nylon (PA) and carbon-fibre nylon</strong> – very hygroscopic; dry before every print.</li>
<li><strong>PC and PVA</strong> – also need drying. Bambu Lab warns that PC can become brittle after many drying and cooling cycles, so do not dry it more often than needed.</li>
<li><strong>PETG</strong> – picks up moisture over days or weeks; drying fixes most stringing problems.</li>
<li><strong>PLA</strong> – absorbs water more slowly. A freshly opened, vacuum-sealed spool usually prints fine.</li>
</ul>
<p>Bambu Lab&#39;s filament table lists drying requirements for each material it sells, and it marks several engineering filaments as needing drying before use.</p>
<h2>How to dry filament with a dryer</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Check the label.</strong> Find the drying temperature and time for your exact filament. If there is none, look up the maker&#39;s datasheet.</li>
<li><strong>Mount the spool.</strong> Bambu Lab asks you to put refill (spool-less) filament on a spool before drying.</li>
<li><strong>Set the temperature and time.</strong> Stay at or below the recommended temperature. Too hot, and the filament can fuse together on the spool.</li>
<li><strong>Let it run fully.</strong> A heavily soaked spool may need the longer end of the recommended time.</li>
<li><strong>Print or store immediately.</strong> Dry filament starts absorbing water again as soon as it cools in room air.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> Weigh a spool before and after drying on a kitchen scale. If the weight drops by a few grams, water was there.</p>
<h2>What not to use</h2>
<p>Bambu Lab advises against drying filament in a microwave or a kitchen oven. A microwave heats unevenly and has no temperature control. A kitchen oven has hot spots near the heating elements that can soften the spool or the filament. A dedicated dryer, or a printer&#39;s own drying function where it has one, gives steadier heat.</p>
<h2>Storing filament after drying</h2>
<ul>
<li>Keep spools in <strong>sealed bags or boxes with desiccant</strong> (silica gel). Replace or recharge the desiccant when its indicator changes colour.</li>
<li>An <strong>AMS or other enclosed feeder with desiccant</strong> keeps spools dry during use, according to Bambu Lab.</li>
<li>A cheap <strong>hygrometer</strong> inside the box tells you when humidity is creeping up.</li>
<li>Label each spool with the date you last dried it.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Choosing a filament dryer</h2>
<p>Look for these features rather than brand names:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Adjustable temperature</strong> that covers the materials you print (higher for nylon and PC).</li>
<li><strong>Airflow or venting</strong>, so moist air leaves the box.</li>
<li><strong>A filament outlet</strong> to print directly from the dryer.</li>
<li><strong>Room for the spool sizes</strong> you use, including 2 kg or cardboard spools.</li>
<li><strong>A timer</strong>, so it switches off on its own.</li>
</ul>
<p>Check the current specifications on the maker&#39;s product page before buying, because models change often.</p>
<h2>What this means for you</h2>
<p>If you print mostly PLA and store spools sealed, start with a storage box and desiccant. If you print TPU, nylon, PC or PETG, or your workshop is humid, a filament dryer pays for itself in prints that no longer fail.</p>
