<h2>What is the difference between ASA and ABS?</h2>
<p>Both are styrene-based plastics with a rubber phase that makes them tough. The difference is the rubber. ABS uses <strong>butadiene</strong>, whose chemical double bonds are broken down by ultraviolet light – that is why ABS parts fade, yellow and crack when left in the sun. ASA swaps it for an <strong>acrylate</strong> rubber, which Polymaker&#39;s material wiki credits for ASA keeping its colour and mechanical properties after long sun exposure.</p>
<p>Prusa&#39;s knowledge base calls ABS the predecessor of ASA, and lists &quot;no UV resistance&quot; among ABS&#39;s main drawbacks. Everything else – stiffness, sanding, acetone behaviour, the need for warmth while printing – is very similar. Polymaker sums up its own ABS and ASA lines as having &quot;extremely similar&quot; mechanical properties and print settings, with ASA winning on weather resistance.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> if a part will stay indoors, the UV advantage is wasted, and you choose between the two on brand, colour and availability. If it goes outside, ASA is the safer default. Our <a href="/blog/asa-filament/">ASA filament guide</a> and <a href="/blog/abs-filament/">ABS filament guide</a> cover each material on its own.</p>

<p>So Bambu Lab&#39;s own ABS sits below its ASA, the two PolyLite filaments are almost level, and Polymaker&#39;s generic table puts ABS ahead. Heat deflection temperature (HDT) also changes with the load used in the test – a figure at 1.8 MPa is always lower than one at 0.45 MPa – so only compare numbers measured the same way. Specialised grades shift the picture again; Polymaker&#39;s ABS Max, for example, is rated at 114 °C HDT.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> all of these values are far above standard PLA, which typically softens around 55–60 °C. For a car interior or a part near a motor, pick the specific filament with the best HDT on its data sheet rather than picking ASA or ABS by name. See <a href="/blog/pla-vs-abs/">PLA vs ABS</a> for how both compare with PLA.</p>
<h2>Is ASA as strong as ABS?</h2>
<p>For everyday parts, yes. Polymaker&#39;s wiki lists notched impact strength of about 180 J/m for ASA and 200 J/m for ABS – a small edge for ABS – and both plastics are stiff and fairly tough. Tensile strength is similar, but the published numbers vary so much between brands and test methods that a ranking would mean little.</p>
<p>In practice, the strength of a printed part depends more on wall count, layer adhesion and print orientation than on the ASA-versus-ABS choice. Both bond best with a warm chamber and little part cooling.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for brackets and clips, add walls before you add infill, and orient the part so the load does not pull layers apart.</p>
<h2>Warping, shrinkage and the enclosure</h2>
<p>Both plastics shrink as they cool, which makes corners lift and tall parts split between layers. Prusa notes ABS parts typically shrink by about 1–2 % and calls warping its main drawback. Prusa describes ASA as warping a little less than ABS, but neither is easy on an open printer. Polymaker recommends an enclosed printer with the air inside above 45 °C for both.</p>
<p>An enclosed printer such as the <a href="/printers/bambu-lab-p1s/">Bambu Lab P1S</a> holds enough heat from its bed for most ASA and ABS parts. On an open printer, keep parts small, add a brim, use the slicer&#39;s draft shield and keep the printer away from fans, windows and air conditioning.</p>
<h2>Smell, fumes and ventilation</h2>
<p>Both release styrene-type fumes and a noticeable smell while printing. Prusa lists potentially dangerous styrene fumes as a drawback of both materials; Bambu Lab and Prusa both describe ASA&#39;s smell as milder than ABS. &quot;Milder&quot; does not mean safe – the advice is the same for both: print in a well-ventilated room or with a filtered enclosure, avoid drafts across the print, and leave the enclosure closed for a few minutes after the print ends.</p>
<h2>Finish and post-processing</h2>
<p>Polymaker describes ASA as having a slightly nicer matte finish that partly hides layer lines; ABS finish varies more between brands. Both:</p>
<ul>
<li>sand easily, wet or dry;</li>
<li>can be smoothed with acetone vapour for a glossy surface;</li>
<li>can be glued with acetone (Prusa warns never to use acetone or ABS/ASA &quot;juice&quot; on a powder-coated print sheet).</li>
</ul>
<p>Neither is food-safe, so do not use either for cups, plates or containers for food.</p>
<h2>Outdoor use: how much does UV resistance matter?</h2>
<p>This is where the choice is clear. Prusa says outdoor ABS parts turn yellowish and more brittle over time; Bambu Lab and Polymaker market their ASA for long-term outdoor use against sunlight, rain and temperature swings. No maker publishes a guaranteed outdoor lifespan for either, so we will not invent one – but for anything mounted outside, ASA is the material designed for the job.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for outdoor parts, add extra walls rather than extra infill – the outer skin takes the weathering. Darker colours get hotter in direct sun, so leave a margin on heat resistance.</p>

<h2>Checklist: switching from ABS to ASA</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Keep your setup:</strong> same enclosure, plate and glue you use for ABS.</li>
<li><strong>Dry the new spool</strong> if it has been open – 80 °C for 8 hours is Bambu Lab&#39;s advice for both.</li>
<li><strong>Load the maker&#39;s ASA profile</strong>, or start from your ABS profile with the nozzle 5 °C higher.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the part fan low or off</strong> for most layers.</li>
<li><strong>Add a brim</strong> to large flat parts and sharp corners.</li>
<li><strong>Preheat with the door closed</strong> so the chamber warms up before the first layer.</li>
<li><strong>Ventilate the room</strong>, just as you would for ABS.</li>
<li><strong>Check the model first</strong> in our <a href="/stl-viewer/">STL viewer</a> for thin walls and large flat bases.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>ASA vs ABS is not a contest of strength or heat – the two are close, and brand-to-brand differences often matter more. The real divider is sunlight: ASA is built to stay strong and keep its colour outdoors, and ABS is not. If you already print ABS on an enclosed printer, switching to ASA for outdoor parts takes nothing more than a new spool and a profile change. For indoor parts, either works; choose by the data sheet, colour and finish you want.</p>
