ASA vs ABS – which filament to choose for tough, heat-resistant parts

Updated November 26, 2026

Key points
  • ASA and ABS print with almost the same settings – Bambu Lab's filament table gives both 240–280 °C nozzle and 90–100 °C bed, with an enclosure recommended.
  • The big difference is sunlight – ASA's acrylate rubber resists UV, while ABS turns yellow and brittle outdoors.
  • Heat resistance and toughness depend on the brand and grade, not on ASA or ABS as such, so compare the data sheets of the filaments you would actually buy.

ASA vs ABS comes down to one main question – will the part see sunlight? ASA resists UV and weather, so it is the better filament for outdoor parts, while ABS prints almost the same way and is a good choice for indoor parts that need heat resistance and toughness. The two plastics are close relatives, need the same kind of printer (hot nozzle, heated bed, enclosure) and give similar strength. This comparison goes further than the usual one-line answer: it puts each maker's ASA and ABS data side by side, explains why "which is more heat-resistant" has no single answer, and ends with a job-by-job choice. Facts come from Bambu Lab, Prusa and Polymaker documentation, checked November 2026.

What is the difference between ASA and ABS?

Both are styrene-based plastics with a rubber phase that makes them tough. The difference is the rubber. ABS uses butadiene, whose chemical double bonds are broken down by ultraviolet light – that is why ABS parts fade, yellow and crack when left in the sun. ASA swaps it for an acrylate rubber, which Polymaker's material wiki credits for ASA keeping its colour and mechanical properties after long sun exposure.

Prusa's knowledge base calls ABS the predecessor of ASA, and lists "no UV resistance" among ABS's main drawbacks. Everything else – stiffness, sanding, acetone behaviour, the need for warmth while printing – is very similar. Polymaker sums up its own ABS and ASA lines as having "extremely similar" mechanical properties and print settings, with ASA winning on weather resistance.

Why it matters: if a part will stay indoors, the UV advantage is wasted, and you choose between the two on brand, colour and availability. If it goes outside, ASA is the safer default. Our ASA filament guide and ABS filament guide cover each material on its own.

ABS vs ASA filament print settings, maker by maker

Most comparisons give one generic range for each plastic. It is more useful to see how the same maker sets up its own ASA and ABS:

Maker ASA ABS
Bambu Lab filament table 240–280 °C nozzle, 90–100 °C bed, glue, enclosed printer recommended 240–280 °C nozzle, 90–100 °C bed, glue, enclosed printer recommended
Prusa material guides 260 °C nozzle, 105 °C first layer then 110 °C bed 255 °C nozzle, 100 °C bed (80–110 °C by part size)
Polymaker PolyLite data sheets 240–260 °C nozzle, 75–95 °C bed, fan off 245–265 °C nozzle, 90–100 °C bed, fan off, closed chamber

Within one brand the gap is a few degrees at most, and Bambu Lab treats them identically. Both need the part cooling fan low or off, a PEI or engineering plate with glue, and dry filament – Bambu Lab gives the same drying advice for both, 80 °C for 8 hours.

Tip: moving from ABS to ASA (or back) does not need a new printer or plate. Switch to the filament maker's profile, print a small calibration part and adjust the nozzle by 5 °C steps if layers split or strings appear.

Heat resistance: why there is no single winner

Many articles say ABS handles more heat. The maker data shows it depends on the grade and the test:

Data source ASA ABS
Bambu Lab (HDT at 0.45 MPa) 100 °C 87 °C
Polymaker PolyLite data sheets (HDT at 0.45 MPa) 102.6 °C 100 °C
Polymaker material wiki, generic grades (HDT) 86–96 °C 100–110 °C

So Bambu Lab's own ABS sits below its ASA, the two PolyLite filaments are almost level, and Polymaker's generic table puts ABS ahead. Heat deflection temperature (HDT) also changes with the load used in the test – a figure at 1.8 MPa is always lower than one at 0.45 MPa – so only compare numbers measured the same way. Specialised grades shift the picture again; Polymaker's ABS Max, for example, is rated at 114 °C HDT.

Why it matters: all of these values are far above standard PLA, which typically softens around 55–60 °C. For a car interior or a part near a motor, pick the specific filament with the best HDT on its data sheet rather than picking ASA or ABS by name. See PLA vs ABS for how both compare with PLA.

Is ASA as strong as ABS?

For everyday parts, yes. Polymaker's wiki lists notched impact strength of about 180 J/m for ASA and 200 J/m for ABS – a small edge for ABS – and both plastics are stiff and fairly tough. Tensile strength is similar, but the published numbers vary so much between brands and test methods that a ranking would mean little.

In practice, the strength of a printed part depends more on wall count, layer adhesion and print orientation than on the ASA-versus-ABS choice. Both bond best with a warm chamber and little part cooling.

Tip: for brackets and clips, add walls before you add infill, and orient the part so the load does not pull layers apart.

Warping, shrinkage and the enclosure

Both plastics shrink as they cool, which makes corners lift and tall parts split between layers. Prusa notes ABS parts typically shrink by about 1–2 % and calls warping its main drawback. Prusa describes ASA as warping a little less than ABS, but neither is easy on an open printer. Polymaker recommends an enclosed printer with the air inside above 45 °C for both.

An enclosed printer such as the Bambu Lab P1S holds enough heat from its bed for most ASA and ABS parts. On an open printer, keep parts small, add a brim, use the slicer's draft shield and keep the printer away from fans, windows and air conditioning.

Smell, fumes and ventilation

Both release styrene-type fumes and a noticeable smell while printing. Prusa lists potentially dangerous styrene fumes as a drawback of both materials; Bambu Lab and Prusa both describe ASA's smell as milder than ABS. "Milder" does not mean safe – the advice is the same for both: print in a well-ventilated room or with a filtered enclosure, avoid drafts across the print, and leave the enclosure closed for a few minutes after the print ends.

Finish and post-processing

Polymaker describes ASA as having a slightly nicer matte finish that partly hides layer lines; ABS finish varies more between brands. Both:

  • sand easily, wet or dry;
  • can be smoothed with acetone vapour for a glossy surface;
  • can be glued with acetone (Prusa warns never to use acetone or ABS/ASA "juice" on a powder-coated print sheet).

Neither is food-safe, so do not use either for cups, plates or containers for food.

Outdoor use: how much does UV resistance matter?

This is where the choice is clear. Prusa says outdoor ABS parts turn yellowish and more brittle over time; Bambu Lab and Polymaker market their ASA for long-term outdoor use against sunlight, rain and temperature swings. No maker publishes a guaranteed outdoor lifespan for either, so we will not invent one – but for anything mounted outside, ASA is the material designed for the job.

Tip: for outdoor parts, add extra walls rather than extra infill – the outer skin takes the weathering. Darker colours get hotter in direct sun, so leave a margin on heat resistance.

Which to choose: ASA or ABS?

Job Better choice Why
Garden, balcony and garage-door fittings ASA UV and weather resistance
Car exterior trim, mirror and sensor mounts ASA Sun plus heat

Indoor electronics enclosures Either No UV exposure; pick by colour and brand
Printer parts and jigs Either Both handle warm enclosures
Parts to be acetone-smoothed Either Both smooth well

Checklist: switching from ABS to ASA

  1. Keep your setup: same enclosure, plate and glue you use for ABS.
  2. Dry the new spool if it has been open – 80 °C for 8 hours is Bambu Lab's advice for both.
  3. Load the maker's ASA profile, or start from your ABS profile with the nozzle 5 °C higher.
  4. Keep the part fan low or off for most layers.
  5. Add a brim to large flat parts and sharp corners.
  6. Preheat with the door closed so the chamber warms up before the first layer.
  7. Ventilate the room, just as you would for ABS.
  8. Check the model first in our STL viewer for thin walls and large flat bases.

Verdict

ASA vs ABS is not a contest of strength or heat – the two are close, and brand-to-brand differences often matter more. The real divider is sunlight: ASA is built to stay strong and keep its colour outdoors, and ABS is not. If you already print ABS on an enclosed printer, switching to ASA for outdoor parts takes nothing more than a new spool and a profile change. For indoor parts, either works; choose by the data sheet, colour and finish you want.

Questions and answers

ASA vs ABS – which is better?

Neither is better overall. ASA is the better choice for parts that live outdoors or in the sun, because it resists UV and weathering. ABS is fine for indoor parts that need heat resistance and toughness, and some ABS grades are tougher or more heat-resistant than standard ASA.

Is ASA as strong as ABS?

Roughly, yes. Polymaker describes their mechanical properties as extremely similar; its material wiki gives ABS a small edge in notched impact strength, while tensile strength is close. Differences between brands are often bigger than the difference between the two plastics.

Is ASA the same as ABS?

No, but they are close relatives. Both contain acrylonitrile and styrene. ABS adds butadiene rubber, which breaks down in sunlight; ASA uses an acrylate rubber instead, which is what makes it UV-stable.

Can I use my ABS profile to print ASA?

Usually as a starting point, yes. Bambu Lab lists the same temperature ranges for both, and Prusa's recommendations differ by only a few degrees. Load the filament maker's own ASA profile if one exists, and fine-tune with a small test print.
