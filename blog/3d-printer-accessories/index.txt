<h2>What comes in the box (check before you buy)</h2>
<p>Makers ship a starter kit with most printers, and it covers more than people expect. Bambu Lab&#39;s wiki lists the P1S accessory box as including a textured PEI plate, a spare 0.4 mm hotend, nozzle wiping pads, an unclogging tool for the 0.4 mm nozzle, a PTFE tube, a Bambu scraper, H1.5 and H2.0 hex keys, thermal grease, lubricant grease, spare extruder screws, double-sided tape and a quick start guide. Bambu Lab also notes that box contents can change between versions. See our <a href="/printers/bambu-lab-p1s/">Bambu Lab P1S page</a> for the printer itself.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> lay out everything from the accessory box on day one, label it, and keep it in one tray near the printer. Many &quot;missing&quot; tools turn out to be in the box.</p>
<h2>Essential hand tools for 3D printing</h2>
<p>These are the 3D printer tools you will use every week:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flush cutters</strong> – snip support material, brims and filament ends cleanly. A square, fresh filament tip loads more easily.</li>
<li><strong>Scraper or print-removal blade</strong> – Polymaker&#39;s wiki recommends a metal scraper with a tapered front for lifting prints. Use it at a shallow angle, push away from your hand, and never gouge a PEI surface.</li>
<li><strong>Deburring tool</strong> – a swivelling blade that removes the sharp edge from holes and the &quot;elephant&#39;s foot&quot; ridge on the first layer in one pass.</li>
<li><strong>Digital caliper</strong> – measures parts, filament diameter and holes, and is the tool you need to check dimensional accuracy or calibrate extrusion. Polymaker lists calipers as useful for measurement and E-step calibration.</li>
<li><strong>Hex keys</strong> – a metric set (Polymaker suggests M2.5 to M5 sizes) plus the small keys from the box. Ball-end keys help in tight spots.</li>
<li><strong>Tweezers and needle-nose pliers</strong> – remove ooze from the nozzle and pull strands out of fans and gears.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> keep a separate pair of cutters for filament and for electronics; cutting filament dulls the edge.</p>
<h2>Spare parts to keep on hand</h2>
<p>A printer that stops for a missing part costs more time than the part. Keep at least one of each:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nozzles or a complete hotend.</strong> Polymaker recommends stocking spare brass or hardened steel nozzles because worn nozzles cause print quality problems. On printers with quick-swap hotends (many Bambu Lab, Prusa and Creality models), a spare hotend is the easiest fix.</li>
<li><strong>PTFE tube and couplers.</strong> Prusa&#39;s guide says to replace deformed or worn PTFE tubes and not to push solid wire through them, which damages the inside.</li>
<li><strong>A spare build plate.</strong> A second plate lets you keep printing while one cools, and covers you if a surface gets damaged. Having two textures (smooth and textured PEI, for example) also changes the look of the bottom surface.</li>
<li><strong>Consumables.</strong> Bambu Lab&#39;s maintenance guide lists the nozzle wiper as something to check before every print and the filament cutter blade as a part to replace every 3–5 rolls of PLA, PETG, ABS or PC, and every 1–2 rolls of carbon- or glass-filled nylon.</li>
<li><strong>Small electronics spares.</strong> Polymaker also suggests spare thermistors, heaters and fans in the correct size and voltage for your printer.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Hardened nozzles: when you need one</h2>
<p>Abrasive filaments wear soft nozzles quickly. Bambu Lab&#39;s filament table marks a hardened steel nozzle as required for carbon- and glass-fibre filled grades – including PLA-CF/GF, ABS-GF, PA-CF/GF, PET-CF/GF, PPA-CF/GF and PPS-CF/GF – and for metal-filled PLA. It says these materials should not be printed with stainless steel or brass nozzles. Prusa gives the same warning for its stock brass nozzle. For PLA, PETG, ABS, ASA and TPU, a brass or stainless nozzle is fine.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> buy a hardened nozzle or hotend only when you plan to print composites. A hardened nozzle in the wrong size or for the wrong hotend is a common wasted purchase – match it to your exact printer model.</p>

<p>Glue also works as a release layer for materials that grip some surfaces too hard. Blue painter&#39;s tape is an older alternative for open-frame printers without PEI.</p>
<p>Cleaning matters more than any adhesive. Bambu Lab&#39;s PEI plate guide recommends washing the textured plate with warm water and dish soap, scrubbing into the texture with a sponge or brush, and handling the plate by its edges because skin oil ruins adhesion. It warns that alcohol can spread oils on a textured plate rather than remove them.</p>
<h2>Filament storage and drying</h2>
<p>Filament absorbs moisture from the air, and wet filament causes stringing, popping and weak parts. Sealed boxes or bags with desiccant cover storage; a filament dryer fixes spools that are already wet. Bambu Lab&#39;s material table gives drying settings such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>PLA: 55 °C for 8 hours</li>
<li>PETG and TPU: 70 °C for 8 hours</li>
<li>ABS and ASA: 80 °C for 8 hours</li>
<li>PA (nylon) and PC: 80 °C for 8–12 hours (PA) or 8 hours (PC), with desiccant required</li>
</ul>
<p>Always use the temperature on your spool&#39;s label if it differs. Our <a href="/blog/filament-dryer/">filament dryer guide</a> explains how to choose and use one.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> a humidity card or small hygrometer inside each storage box tells you when to replace the desiccant.</p>
<h2>Enclosure, filtration and extraction</h2>
<p>An enclosure keeps heat in for materials that warp and keeps drafts off the print. Bambu Lab&#39;s table recommends an enclosed printer for ABS, ASA, PC and nylon (and their CF/GF variants), says an enclosure is &quot;more recommended&quot; for PETG, and lists PLA, PLA-CF and TPU as fine on open-frame printers.</p>
<p>Filters are a related accessory. Bambu Lab&#39;s X1 series uses an activated carbon filter that cleans enclosure air before it leaves the printer, and Bambu Lab recommends replacing it after 1,440 hours of printing (about 60 days), or every three months at around 8 hours a day. If you print ABS or ASA often, also plan where the air goes: a ventilated room or extraction to the outside.</p>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> if you build a DIY enclosure, keep the power supply outside it – Prusa&#39;s enclosure guide warns the inside can get warmer than the power supply&#39;s recommended operating temperature, and do not seal an open printer inside a cupboard without ventilation.</p>
<h2>Maintenance supplies and upgrades</h2>
<p>A small maintenance kit prevents most failures:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and a lint-free cloth.</strong> Bambu Lab uses IPA and a dust-free cloth to clean the X1&#39;s carbon rods and linear rods monthly (every 5 rolls if you print ABS or ASA), and to clean camera lenses.</li>
<li><strong>Grease and light oil.</strong> Bambu Lab lists grease for the Z lead screws every three months and names suitable products such as Super Lube 92003; it says not to grease the X-axis carbon rods. Prusa suggests re-lubricating smooth rods after about 200 hours of printing and says not to lubricate the Z-axis trapezoid nut. Polymaker mentions white lithium grease for rods. Use what your printer&#39;s maker specifies.</li>
<li><strong>Compressed air and a brass brush.</strong> Prusa uses compressed air for fans and extruder gears and a brass brush on a hot nozzle; Bambu Lab cleans extruder gears and fans with compressed air weekly.</li>
<li><strong>Nozzle cleaning needles.</strong> Useful for clogs, together with cold pulls. See <a href="/blog/how-to-clean-a-3d-printer-nozzle/">how to clean a 3D printer nozzle</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Upgrades come after maintenance. Common ones are a second build plate, a hardened hotend, a filament dryer, a multi-colour unit, anti-vibration feet and a sturdy <a href="/blog/3d-printer-stand/">3D printer stand</a>. Buy them to solve a problem you actually have.</p>
<h2>Safety accessories</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Smoke detector.</strong> MatterHackers&#39; fire safety guide advises a smoke detector in the same room as the printer, tested regularly.</li>
<li><strong>Fire extinguisher.</strong> The same guide notes that a printer fire is an electrical fire and recommends a Class C extinguisher within reach. Polymaker mentions a fire extinguishing ball mounted above the printer as an extra measure.</li>
<li><strong>Gloves and safety glasses.</strong> Purdue University&#39;s 3D printer safety guidance calls for gloves and eye protection when handling resin or heated components.</li>
<li><strong>Heat-resistant gloves</strong> for nozzle and hotend work, which Bambu Lab lists for nozzle cleaning.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not leave a printer running when nobody is in the building – both MatterHackers and Purdue advise against unattended printing.</p>
<h2>Post-processing tools</h2>
<p>If you finish parts, add:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sandpaper in several grits (wet-and-dry paper works well on PLA and resin).</li>
<li>Needle files and a hobby knife for small details.</li>
<li>A rotary tool for trimming and polishing – use low speed on PLA, which softens from friction heat.</li>
<li>Glue for repairs and assemblies; Polymaker suggests super glue gel for minor PLA repairs.</li>
<li>Primer and paint if you plan to paint models.</li>
</ul>
<p>For resin printers, the list changes: nitrile gloves, isopropyl alcohol for washing, a resin filter and a curing light or station.</p>
<h2>How to build your 3D printer accessory kit – checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li>Unpack and list everything from the printer&#39;s accessory box.</li>
<li>Add flush cutters, a deburring tool, a digital caliper, tweezers and a metric hex key set.</li>
<li>Buy one spare nozzle or hotend for your exact printer model, a spare PTFE tube and a second build plate.</li>
<li>Get sealed filament storage with desiccant; add a dryer if you print PETG, TPU or nylon.</li>
<li>Put together a maintenance kit: IPA, lint-free cloth, the grease your maker specifies, compressed air and a brass brush.</li>
<li>Fit a smoke detector in the printer&#39;s room and keep a Class C extinguisher nearby.</li>
<li>Add a glue stick if your materials or plates need it.</li>
<li>Only then consider upgrades: hardened hotend for composites, an enclosure and filter for ABS and ASA, a stand, a multi-colour unit.</li>
</ol>
<p>To check models before you print them, open them in our <a href="/stl-viewer/">STL viewer</a>.</p>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>You do not need a large toolbox. Start with what came in the box, add cutters, a caliper, a deburring tool, a spare nozzle and plate, dry storage and basic maintenance supplies, and fit a smoke detector. Buy a hardened nozzle, an enclosure, filters and upgrades only when the material or problem in front of you calls for them – and follow your printer maker&#39;s maintenance guide for which lubricants and cleaners to use.</p>
