This guide explains what an AMS does, how a filament switch works, the four current models, what they cannot handle and how other makers&#39; systems compare. Facts come from the Bambu Lab Wiki, blog and store, checked November 2026.</p>

<h2>What is AMS 3D printing?</h2>
<p>A normal desktop FDM printer has one filament path: one spool, one extruder, one nozzle. To print in two colours you would stop the printer and change the spool by hand. An AMS automates that swap. Bambu Lab describes the original AMS as three main parts:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Feeders</strong> – four slots, each with its own motor and gears that push filament forward or wind it back onto the spool, plus a sensor that detects the filament.</li>
<li><strong>Hub</strong> – merges the four filament paths into one, with Hall sensors, a magnetic rotary encoder (used as an odometer) and a brushless motor that gives a second push.</li>
<li><strong>Buffer</strong> – sits between the AMS and the toolhead and keeps the filament tension in range, so the extruder is not fighting the spool.</li>
</ul>
<p>The wiki says the original AMS has 10 sensors along the filament path. The unit is sealed, with desiccant and gaskets, so it also works as a dry box for stored spools.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> an AMS turns a single-colour printer into a multi-colour one without a second nozzle. It also lets you keep four materials loaded and pick one in the slicer instead of swapping spools.</p>
<h2>How an AMS works: feeding and switching filament</h2>
<p>When you push a filament end into a slot, the feeder senses it and pulls it in until the hub detects it. When a print starts, the feeder drives the filament towards the hub, then the hub motor takes over and pushes it all the way to the toolhead. Once the extruder grips it, the AMS switches to &quot;feed assist&quot; mode and only adds force when the buffer shows tension building up.</p>
<p>A colour change on a single-nozzle Bambu printer then goes roughly like this:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Cut</strong> – a filament cutter on the toolhead cuts off the current filament at the toolhead.</li>
<li><strong>Retract</strong> – the feeder and hub motor wind the old filament back onto its spool.</li>
<li><strong>Load</strong> – the new filament is driven from its slot up to the extruder.</li>
<li><strong>Purge</strong> – the printer pushes enough new filament through the nozzle to clear the old colour. On the X1 series the purged strand drops out of the back through a waste chute, past a nozzle wiper, before printing continues.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> each change takes time, so a model with many colour changes per layer can take much longer than the single-colour version. Bambu Studio shows the extra time and filament in its slicing estimate – check it before you print.</p>
<h2>Purge, flushing volume and the prime tower</h2>
<p>Bambu Lab calls the purged filament the <strong>flushing volume</strong>: the amount of new filament needed to push the old colour out so the model is not stained. It depends on the colour pair – the wiki says changing from black to white needs several times more filament than white to black. Bambu Studio calculates it automatically from the &quot;from&quot; and &quot;to&quot; colours.</p>
<p>Bambu Studio offers three ways to put some of that purge into the print instead of the bin:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flush into infill</strong> – the hidden inside of the model absorbs the colour transition (not a good idea with transparent filament).</li>
<li><strong>Flush into support</strong> – supports are removed anyway, so they can take mixed colours.</li>
<li><strong>Flush into object</strong> – a chosen part, such as an internal gear, is printed from the transition material.</li>
</ul>
<p>The support and object options only work when the <strong>prime tower</strong> is enabled. The prime tower is a small block printed next to the model that primes the nozzle after each change and keeps the outer walls clean.</p>
<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> purged filament is the main hidden cost of single-nozzle multi-colour printing. Group colours by layer where you can, and use flush-into-infill on large models.</p>
<h2>RFID, auto refill and filament tracking</h2>
<p>Bambu Lab spools carry RFID tags on both sides. The AMS reads them with built-in RFID boards and sends the filament type and colour to Bambu Studio and the printer, so slots are recognised even if you load them in random order. The wiki says the AMS estimates remaining filament from how much is fed during one full spool rotation, with a full 1 kg spool counted as 100 %. Our <a href="/blog/bambu-filament/">Bambu filament guide</a> covers the RFID-tagged range.</p>
<p>Third-party filament works too – you set its material and colour for that slot by hand in the slicer or on the printer screen.</p>
<p><strong>AMS Auto-Refill</strong> switches to another slot with the same filament type and colour when a spool runs out. You turn it on in the AMS settings. The wiki warns that pausing and unloading mid-print can disrupt this backup function.</p>

</ul>
<p><strong>Which one you need:</strong> an A1 or A1 mini owner uses the AMS lite; a <a href="/printers/bambu-lab-p1s/">P1S</a> or X1 owner can use the AMS, AMS 2 Pro or AMS HT; H2 printers are designed around the AMS 2 Pro and AMS HT.</p>
<h2>What does AMS mean for filament choice? Limits to know</h2>
<p>The AMS feeds filament through long PTFE tubes and pushes it with gears, so the filament has to be stiff and robust enough to survive the trip. Bambu Lab&#39;s notes on using the AMS list:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flexible filament:</strong> do not load TPU, TPE or similar soft filaments – they can buckle and jam in the feed tube. (Bambu Lab lists a special &quot;TPU for AMS&quot; as supported on the AMS 2 Pro; generic TPU and Bambu TPU 95A are not.)</li>
<li><strong>Damp PVA and BVOH:</strong> absorbent support materials can swell and stick; only load them dry.</li>
<li><strong>Fibre-filled and brittle filaments:</strong> third-party PLA-CF, PETG-CF, PA-CF and glass-filled grades, some silk PLA and glow-in-the-dark PLA can wear the PTFE tubes or snap. Bambu Lab says its own PLA-CF, PETG-CF and PAHT-CF are tuned for AMS use.</li>
<li><strong>Spool size:</strong> the AMS takes spools 50–68 mm wide and 197–202 mm in diameter. The AMS lite accepts 40–68 mm widths.</li>
<li><strong>Cardboard spools:</strong> Bambu Lab recommends an adapter ring, because bare cardboard can slip and leave fibres inside the unit. Plastic spools that fit correctly are preferred. The AMS lite&#39;s spring-loaded holders grip the spool hole, which Bambu Lab says improves cardboard compatibility.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Tip:</strong> for anything on the &quot;do not load&quot; list, use the printer&#39;s external spool holder. On H2 and X1/P1 printers with an AMS HT, the bypass outlet is the other option for TPU.</p>

<h2>Do you need an AMS? A quick checklist</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>You print mostly single-colour functional parts:</strong> you do not need one. An external spool holder is enough.</li>
<li><strong>You want multi-colour models, signs, keychains or figures:</strong> an AMS (or AMS lite on the A1) is the simplest way.</li>
<li><strong>You print soluble or breakaway supports in a second material:</strong> an AMS lets you load the support filament next to the model filament.</li>
<li><strong>You print long jobs and hate run-outs:</strong> auto refill from a matching spool is useful even for single-colour prints.</li>
<li><strong>You print damp-sensitive materials (PA, PC, PETG in humid rooms):</strong> look at the drying units – AMS 2 Pro or AMS HT – or a separate filament dryer.</li>
<li><strong>You print mostly TPU:</strong> an AMS will not help; keep it on the external holder.</li>
</ol>
<p>Before buying, open a multi-colour 3MF project in our <a href="/3mf-viewer/">3MF viewer</a> to see how many colours your favourite models really use.</p>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>AMS is Bambu Lab&#39;s Automatic Material System – a filament changer that turns a single-nozzle printer into a multi-colour, multi-material machine and adds automatic spool refill. It works well with rigid filaments such as PLA, PETG, ABS and ASA, but every colour change costs time and purged filament, and soft or brittle filaments are better fed from an external spool. Choose the model by your printer: AMS lite for the A1 series, the AMS or AMS 2 Pro for P1 and X1, and the AMS 2 Pro plus AMS HT for the H2 series.</p>
