Mini 3D printer – what you get from a small printer and how to choose one

Updated November 26, 2026

Key points
  • A mini 3D printer usually has a build volume of about 180 mm per side or less, compared with about 220–256 mm on a typical full-size home printer.
  • Popular small FDM printers such as the Bambu Lab A1 mini and Original Prusa MINI+ both print 180 × 180 × 180 mm, while kids' and toy printers can be far smaller.
  • A small printer saves desk space and is easy to move, but limits part size and can take up nearly as much room as a larger model once the spool and moving bed are counted.

A mini 3D printer is a compact printer with a build volume of about 180 mm per side or less – smaller than the 220 to 256 mm of a typical home printer – that trades part size for less desk space, a lower weight and, often, a simpler start. Small printers are popular as first printers, second printers for quick jobs, and printers for kids, classrooms and dorm rooms. This guide shows the real build volumes and footprints of small printers from their makers' spec pages, explains what fits and what does not, covers the trade-offs that listings rarely mention and ends with a checklist. Specs are from the manufacturers, checked November 2026; prices are not compared.

What counts as a mini 3D printer

There is no official definition. In practice, listings call a printer "mini" when its build volume is clearly below the common 220 mm class. The most common size is the 180 mm cube shared by the Bambu Lab A1 mini and the Original Prusa MINI+. Below that are enclosed compact printers around 150 mm, and kids' printers that often advertise around 100 mm per side or less.

Why it matters: a 180 mm printer and a 100 mm printer are very different tools. The first prints most everyday objects; the second is mainly for toys and keychains.

What fits on a 180 mm bed – and what does not

A 180 × 180 × 180 mm build volume holds a lot more than it sounds. Most figurines, 3D printed toys, fidgets, keychains, desk organisers, plant pots, cable clips, replacement knobs and phone stands fit easily. Long articulated models can often be printed curled up on the plate.

What does not fit in one piece: cosplay helmets, tall vases and lamp shades, long shelf brackets, large storage boxes and most furniture parts. You can split large models and glue them, but every split adds work and a visible seam.

Tip: before you buy, open a few models you want to print in our STL viewer and check their dimensions against the build volume. If more than a handful are too big, a full-size printer is the better choice.

Footprint: smaller, but not as small as you think

A bed-slinger printer moves its bed forward and back, so it needs free space in front of and behind the frame. The Bambu Lab A1 mini lists a printer size of 347 × 315 × 365 mm – far from tiny compared with its 180 mm build volume. Add a side-mounted spool or a multi-colour feeder and the space needed grows again.

Why it matters: if you are short of space, measure the shelf with the bed at both ends of its travel and leave room for the spool. An enclosed compact printer keeps all movement inside its box, which can make it easier to place.

Speed and print quality

Small does not have to mean slow or low-quality. Bambu Lab lists a maximum toolhead speed of 500 mm/s and acceleration of 10,000 mm/s² for the A1 mini – the same class of motion as many full-size printers. The Prusa MINI+ uses the same kind of slicer profiles and bed levelling as larger Prusa printers. Quality on a small printer depends far more on calibration, filament and slicer settings than on the size of the bed.

On the other hand, very cheap toy printers usually have simpler hotends, smaller fans and fewer sensors, so expect slower prints and more limits.

Materials: what a small printer can and cannot print

Material range depends on temperatures and enclosure, not on size:

  • Open-frame mini printers print PLA, PETG and TPU well. Bambu Lab lists an 80 °C bed for the A1 mini, which suits PLA and PETG but is not ideal for ABS or ASA.
  • The Prusa MINI+ has a 100 °C bed and Prusa lists a long range of filaments, but warping-prone materials still benefit from an enclosure.
  • Enclosed compact printers such as the Flashforge Adventurer 3 keep heat in for ABS, although Flashforge lists only PLA and ABS for that model.
  • Kids' printers such as Toybox are designed for PLA only.

Tip: if you mainly print PLA – the right choice for most beginners – any good mini printer will do. Our PLA filament guide covers settings and brands.

Multi-colour on a small printer

Some mini printers can print in several colours. Bambu Lab sells the AMS lite feeder for the A1 mini, which switches between up to four filaments. Multi-colour printing on a small bed works well for signs, badges and small figures, but the purge waste per colour change is the same as on a big printer, so small multi-colour parts produce a relatively large amount of waste.

Resin mini printers

Most resin printers are small by nature. The Elegoo Mars 3, for example, has a build volume of 143 × 89.6 × 175 mm. They are excellent for tabletop miniatures and fine details but need resin handling, washing, curing and ventilation. A small resin printer is not a substitute for a small FDM printer – it is a different tool for a different job.

Mini 3D printers for kids

Printers made for children focus on safety and simplicity: small build volumes, enclosed or guarded hotends, PLA only and app-based control with model libraries. The Toybox Alpha Three is one example. For older children and teens, a capable 180 mm printer with automatic levelling often lasts longer because it can grow with their projects.

Why it matters: whatever printer you choose for a child, the hot nozzle and moving parts mean adult supervision is still needed.

Pros and cons of a mini 3D printer

Pros

  • Takes up less space and is light enough to move or take to a class or club.
  • Often simpler to set up, with fewer parts to assemble.
  • Heats up quickly and uses less power for its heated bed.
  • A good second printer for quick, small jobs while a big printer runs long prints.

Cons

  • Limits part size; large models must be split.
  • Fewer plates of small parts fit per print.
  • Some very small toy printers have limited materials and slow speeds.
  • The footprint, including the moving bed and spool, can be closer to a full-size printer than expected.

Checklist: choosing a mini 3D printer

  1. List what you want to print and check the largest dimensions against the build volume.
  2. Measure your space with the bed's full travel and the spool included.
  3. Decide on materials. PLA and PETG only, or ABS and ASA too? The latter needs a hotter bed and ideally an enclosure.
  4. Look for automatic bed levelling and filament runout detection to save failed prints.
  5. Check the software. A good slicer profile and an app or network printing make a small printer much easier to live with.
  6. Think about colour. If you want multi-colour, check whether the printer supports a feeder.
  7. Compare with a budget full-size printer. Our guide to cheap 3D printers covers larger options that may cost about the same.

You can also line up specs side by side with our printer comparison tool.

Verdict

A mini 3D printer is a sensible choice when space is tight, when you want a simple first printer or when you need a second machine for small jobs. A 180 mm printer such as the Bambu Lab A1 mini or Original Prusa MINI+ prints most everyday objects well; smaller toy printers are best kept for children's projects. If you already know you want to print helmets, large vases or furniture parts, skip the mini class and choose a full-size printer from the start.

Questions and answers

What is a mini 3D printer?

A mini 3D printer is a compact printer with a smaller build volume than a standard home model – usually about 180 mm per side or less. Most are FDM printers that use 1.75 mm filament; small resin printers exist too, with tiny build plates for detailed miniatures.

Is a mini 3D printer good for beginners?

It can be. Small printers such as the Bambu Lab A1 mini and Original Prusa MINI+ have automatic bed levelling and good slicer profiles, which make the first prints easier. The main limit is part size, so check that the things you want to print fit on a 180 mm bed.

What can you print on a mini 3D printer?

Most everyday prints fit – figurines, toys, fidgets, keychains, cable clips, small organisers, replacement knobs, phone stands and print-in-place models. Helmets, large vases and long brackets do not fit in one piece and must be split or printed on a bigger machine.

What is the smallest 3D printer?

Among the printers on our site, the Toybox Alpha Three has the smallest build volume at 75 × 80 × 90 mm, according to Toybox. It is designed for children printing small toys rather than general use.
