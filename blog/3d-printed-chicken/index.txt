<h2>Articulated and flexi 3D printed chickens</h2>
<p>Flexi chickens and chicks are print-in-place models whose segments are separated by small gaps, so the body, neck and tail move as soon as you take them off the plate. They make good desk toys and gifts and are one of the easiest chicken prints.</p>
<ul>
<li>Turn supports off; the model is designed to print without them.</li>
<li>Use a layer height of 0.2 mm or thinner and print at the designed scale, because shrinking the model also shrinks the joint gaps.</li>
<li>Turn on elephant foot compensation so the first layer does not fuse the bottom of the joints.</li>
</ul>
<p>Gradient or multicolour PLA gives a flexi chick a rainbow body with no extra work. For more on joint gaps and fused segments, see our <a href="/blog/3d-printed-animals/">3D printed animals</a> guide, and for other print-in-place playthings, our 3D printed toys article.</p>
<h2>Chicken figurines and low-poly hens</h2>
<p>Low-poly chickens are built from flat triangular faces and usually need no support, so they scale up or down freely. Silk PLA catches the light on each facet; matte PLA gives a clean, modern look. Three walls keep the flat faces crisp.</p>
<p>Realistic hens and roosters need more care. Beaks, combs, wattles, tail feathers and thin legs create overhangs and tiny details.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Orientation:</strong> stand the bird on its feet or base so the face and comb point up or sideways, where support marks matter least.</li>
<li><strong>Layer height:</strong> 0.08–0.12 mm brings out feather detail; 0.16 mm is a common compromise for larger figures.</li>
<li><strong>Supports:</strong> the Bambu Lab Wiki recommends tree or hybrid supports for complex shapes with mostly small, curved overhangs, and advises a top Z distance of about 0.2 mm when supports use the same filament as the model, so they come off cleanly.</li>
<li><strong>Thin legs:</strong> add a brim, or choose a design where the legs join a solid base.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Egg holders, egg cups and planters</h2>
<p>Chicken-shaped egg cups, countertop egg trays and planters turn a chicken model into something useful. They usually print upright with few or no supports.</p>
<ul>
<li>Eggs in their shells are low-risk, but a printed part is not automatically food safe: layer lines trap dirt and are hard to clean. Read <a href="/blog/is-pla-food-safe/">is PLA food safe</a> before printing anything that touches food.</li>
<li>For planters, use 3–4 walls and 4 or more bottom layers, add a drainage hole or use the print as a cover around a plastic nursery pot, and choose PETG for pots that sit outdoors.</li>
</ul>
<h2>3D printed chicken coop accessories</h2>
<p>Backyard keepers print a range of practical parts. The BackYard Chickens community describes printed feed and grit holders that clip to wire mesh, feed scoops with lids, weather-resistant grit and oyster-shell bins with drainage holes, feeder ports sized for roosters with large combs, and a pass-through for an extension cord to run a heated waterer. Model libraries also list chick feeders and chick waterers. Prusa&#39;s farmstead article shows a semi-automatic coop door, where a pedal releases a latch when a hen steps on it and a counterweight opens the door.</p>
<p>Printed parts in a run have to survive beaks, scratching and weather. Guidance from the BackYard Chickens article:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Walls:</strong> use thicker walls and extra wall loops or denser infill, because a chicken&#39;s pecking force is concentrated at the beak tip.</li>
<li><strong>Edges:</strong> smooth any sharp edges or stray strings that could catch combs and wattles.</li>
<li><strong>Fit:</strong> leave room for combs and avoid holes a hen can push her head too far into.</li>
<li><strong>Stability:</strong> hang or anchor light feeders so birds jumping on them cannot tip them over.</li>
<li><strong>Water:</strong> a printed container only holds water if it is printed very smoothly; check for leaks before use.</li>
<li><strong>Cleaning:</strong> wipe or hand-wash with soap and a soft sponge, dry thoroughly, avoid soaking parts that are not 100 % infill, and keep PLA out of the dishwasher, where heat can warp it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Painting and colouring 3D printed chickens</h2>
<p>For simple colour, add a filament change at chosen layers. The Prusa Knowledge Base explains that the printer pauses at the M600 command and asks you to swap filament – useful for a red comb on top of a white hen. A multi-material unit can colour the beak, comb and eyes automatically.</p>
<p>For the most control, paint. All3DP&#39;s painting guide suggests this order for PLA:</p>
<ol>
<li>Trim support stubs with a craft knife or flush cutters.</li>
<li>Sand lightly, starting around 120–220 grit, and go gently around the beak and comb.</li>
<li>Prime with two light coats; paint does not grip bare PLA well.</li>
<li>Paint with acrylics in thin coats – white, brown or black for feathers, red for the comb and wattles, yellow or orange for the beak and feet.</li>
<li>Seal with a clear coat.</li>
</ol>
<p>Paint only decorative pieces, not parts that go into the run.</p>
<h2>Is 3D printed chicken meat real?</h2>
<p>Many recent searches for &quot;3D printed chicken&quot; follow viral videos about printed meat. In short:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Campbell&#39;s</strong> stated in its FAQ that it does not use 3D printed chicken, lab-grown chicken or any artificial or bioengineered meat in its soups, after a recording of a company executive spread online (reported by 3DPrint.com).</li>
<li><strong>Food 3D printing does exist</strong> as a separate field, using specialised food printers rather than hobby machines.</li>
<li><strong>Home printers cannot do this.</strong> Food printers are specialised machines; a home FDM printer melts plastic and is not suitable for food.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How to print your first 3D printed chicken</h2>
<ol>
<li>Choose a flexi chick or a low-poly hen marked &quot;no supports&quot;.</li>
<li>Open the file in our <a href="/3d-viewer/">3D viewer</a> or STL viewer to check its size and that it is one closed model.</li>
<li>Slice in PLA at 0.2 mm with supports off, or follow the designer&#39;s notes.</li>
<li>Watch the first layer go down, then let the plate cool before removing the print.</li>
<li>Flex the joints gently, starting at the tail.</li>
<li>Next, try a figurine with tree supports and 0.12 mm layers, or a feeder in PETG.</li>
</ol>
<h2>Where to find chicken models</h2>
<p>Printables has a dedicated chickens tag, and MakerWorld, Thingiverse and Cults all have chicken figurines, flexi chicks and coop parts. Useful search terms are &quot;flexi chicken&quot;, &quot;articulated chick&quot;, &quot;low poly hen&quot;, &quot;egg holder&quot;, &quot;chicken planter&quot;, &quot;chick feeder&quot; and &quot;grit feeder&quot;. Check each model&#39;s licence before selling prints. To turn a photo of your own flock into a backlit keepsake, our <a href="/lithophane-maker/">lithophane maker</a> builds a printable panel in your browser.</p>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>3D printed chickens range from a quick, support-free flexi chick to useful coop parts. Start with a support-free flexi or low-poly chicken in PLA, move on to figurines with tree supports, and print anything for the run in plain PETG or PLA with thick walls and smooth edges. And if a video says your supermarket chicken came off a 3D printer – that is a different technology, and not something your home printer can do.</p>
